# 1954–1975: hai nhà nước, civil conflict và Global Cold War

## Điểm tựa và câu hỏi trung tâm

[`19_revolution_first_indochina_war_and_geneva.md`](19_revolution_first_indochina_war_and_geneva.md) kết thúc với a provisional military division near the 17th parallel. Điều xảy ra sau đó không phải simply “Mỹ thay Pháp”.

Câu hỏi trung tâm của chapter là: **vì sao temporary regrouping hardened into two rival states, và vì sao conflict giữa Vietnamese actors trở thành một trong những major international wars của Cold War?**

Modern scholarship emphasizes that conflict này có nhiều layer cùng lúc: struggle over national reunification, civil war, revolutionary war, anticommunist state-building và global Cold War intervention. Không layer nào một mình giải thích toàn bộ.

## Hai state-building project sau Geneva

Ở north, **Democratic Republic of Vietnam (DRV)** consolidated a socialist revolutionary state headquartered in Hanoi.

Ở south, **State of Vietnam** transitioned into **Republic of Vietnam (RVN)** under Ngô Đình Diệm after 1955, with increasing United States support and declining direct French influence.

Both governments claimed national legitimacy beyond the territory they controlled. Vì vậy division was not treated by either as an ideal permanent settlement.

## Population movement làm division thành lived experience

Trong 300-day regrouping period, hundreds of thousands moved between zones, with a particularly large flow from north to south including many Catholics, anti-communists and others concerned about life under DRV rule.

Movement changed demography, land demand, church/community networks and politics in southern Vietnam.

Migration is not a footnote. When population chooses or is pressured to move, border becomes social reality.

## North Vietnam: socialist transformation and state penetration

DRV pursued land reform, collectivization over time, industrial planning and expansion of party-state institutions. These policies aimed to transform class/property relations and increase state capacity.

Land reform redistributed property but also involved wrongful classification, coercion and executions in a number of localities; correction campaigns followed.

Later agricultural collectivization reorganized production and rural administration, with mixed outcomes across time and place.

For this library, key mechanism is **state penetration**: household, village and production increasingly linked to party-state organizations, planning and mobilization.

## South Vietnam: nation-building under insecurity

Ngô Đình Diệm's government sought to consolidate authority against sects, rival political networks, remaining French influence and communist underground organization.

State-building involved army reorganization, bureaucracy, refugee resettlement and rural security programs. It also involved authoritarian repression of opponents and restrictions on political competition.

Supporters viewed centralization as necessary for survival; critics saw it as exclusionary and coercive. Both perspectives must be connected to documented institutional practices rather than reduced to labels.

## Why nationwide elections did not occur in 1956

The Geneva political declaration envisaged 1956 elections, but legal/political commitment was contested because the southern State of Vietnam and United States had not signed the Final Declaration in the same manner as parties to the armistice framework.

South Vietnamese leadership rejected elections under conditions it argued could not be free in the north; DRV insisted reunification/elections were part of Geneva political framework.

The result was not peaceful reunification. Separate state institutions deepened and mutual distrust rose.

## Insurgency in the South: local roots + northern strategy

By late 1950s, armed conflict in South Vietnam intensified. Former Viet Minh networks, local grievances, government repression and decisions by Hanoi leadership all mattered.

The **National Liberation Front (NLF)** was formed in 1960, bringing together communist-led and other anti-Saigon elements under a broad front structure.

Avoid two oversimplifications: it was neither purely spontaneous southern rebellion disconnected from Hanoi nor merely external invasion with no southern social base.

```text
local political/social grievances
      + underground networks
      + Hanoi strategy/support
      ↓
expanding insurgency
```

## United States involvement escalates

US involvement grew from financial/military support and advisers in the 1950s to much larger direct military commitment in the 1960s.

Cold War doctrine, fear of communist expansion, alliance credibility and South Vietnamese state survival all shaped US decisions.

Escalation was gradual and path-dependent: each commitment changed cost of withdrawing and expectation of partners/opponents.

## 1963 crisis: state legitimacy matters as much as military hardware

Tensions between Diệm government and Buddhist activists escalated in 1963. Political crisis, repression and military dissatisfaction culminated in a coup in November 1963 in which Diệm and Ngô Đình Nhu were killed.

Subsequent governments in South Vietnam experienced repeated political turnover before greater stability later in the decade.

Lesson: external aid cannot substitute for domestic coalition management. Army size and equipment are only one dimension of state capacity.

## 1965: large-scale US ground war and North Vietnamese regular forces

From 1965, United States deployed large ground forces and conducted sustained air campaigns. North Vietnam increased infiltration and deployment of regular forces to the south while continuing support for NLF/People's Liberation Armed Forces.

War now operated at multiple scales: village-level insurgency, conventional battles, air war over North Vietnam, Ho Chi Minh trail/logistics through Laos/Cambodia, and international diplomacy.

The theater cannot be understood inside modern Vietnam borders alone.

## Ho Chi Minh trail: logistics system, not one road

The so-called **Ho Chi Minh trail** was a network of roads, paths, pipelines and supply nodes through Laos and Cambodia as well as Vietnam.

Its historical significance is systems resilience. If one route is bombed, traffic can reroute; repair crews restore bridges/roads; camouflage and dispersed storage reduce vulnerability.

```text
network redundancy + labor + repair
       ↓
supply survives interdiction
       ↓
forces in south remain connected to north
```

Again, logistics converts political intention into military capability.

## War economy and destruction

War transformed both economies. South Vietnam received massive foreign aid and military spending, accelerating urbanization and service sectors while also creating dependency and inflationary/distortion risks.

North Vietnam mobilized population and production under bombing, dispersing industry and relying heavily on Soviet/Chinese assistance.

Rural communities across conflict zones faced displacement, crop destruction, insecurity and forced/strategic relocation.

GDP-style aggregate alone cannot describe wartime welfare.

## 1968 Tet Offensive: military, political and media outcomes differ

During **Tet 1968**, communist forces launched widespread attacks across South Vietnam. Militarily, attackers suffered heavy losses and failed to hold most urban objectives for long. Politically and psychologically, the offensive had major consequences, especially for perceptions in the United States about progress and war duration.

This is a useful distinction:

```text
battlefield outcome ≠ strategic political effect
```

A campaign can lose tactically while changing negotiation/public opinion environment—or vice versa.

## The war was internationalized far beyond US–Vietnam

China and Soviet Union supplied major aid to North Vietnam while managing their own Sino-Soviet rivalry. South Korea, Australia, New Zealand, Thailand, Philippines and others contributed forces/support on US/RVN side in different forms. Laos and Cambodia were deeply affected by cross-border war.

Thus “Vietnam War” is geographically convenient but analytically incomplete.

Recent Cambridge scholarship explicitly treats it as global Cold War, civil war and national liberation conflict simultaneously.

## Vietnamization and changing force structure

Under US President Richard Nixon, policy shifted toward **Vietnamization**: expanding responsibility of Republic of Vietnam Armed Forces while reducing US ground troop presence, alongside continued air power and aid.

This changed burden distribution but did not end fighting. Conflict expanded dramatically in Cambodia/Laos contexts and remained connected to US–China–Soviet diplomacy.

## 1973 Paris Peace Accords: US withdrawal does not equal war termination

Paris Peace Accords in **January 1973** produced a ceasefire framework and withdrawal of US combat forces, while leaving Vietnamese armed actors in place and unresolved political competition in the South.

Fighting continued. This is another example where diplomatic agreement changes system constraints without automatically solving underlying sovereignty dispute.

## 1975: rapid conventional collapse

In early 1975, North Vietnamese forces launched major offensives. After breakthroughs in Central Highlands and central coast, RVN military position deteriorated rapidly. Command decisions, logistics, morale, territorial overextension and changing expectations of US support all interacted.

Saigon fell to communist-led forces on **30 April 1975**.

The date is a clear military-political endpoint for the war, but naming varies by memory community: “Liberation of the South/Reunification” in official Vietnamese state narrative; “Fall of Saigon” in much international and overseas Vietnamese usage. The event is the same; labels encode different historical memory and political experience.

## 1975 vs 1976: military end và formal state reunification

1975 ended the Republic of Vietnam and major war. Formal institutional reunification followed in **1976** with establishment of the Socialist Republic of Vietnam.

Keeping these dates separate avoids a common compression error.

## Human cost and displacement

The war killed very large numbers of Vietnamese civilians and combatants, as well as foreign soldiers, and caused massive internal displacement. Bombing, artillery, mines, massacres, imprisonment and postwar reprisals affected communities differently.

Exact aggregate casualty totals vary by source and methodology, so this chapter avoids false precision unless a specific dataset is being discussed.

Human history should not disappear under military chronology.

## War memory: one event, multiple memory systems

Sites such as **Dinh Độc Lập/Reunification Palace**, **Địa đạo Củ Chi**, **Khe Sanh**, **Quảng Trị**, **Hỏa Lò**, war cemeteries and overseas memorials encode different aspects of conflict.

A museum is not neutral raw evidence; exhibition design reflects institutional memory. That does not make museums useless. It means visitor should separate artifact, documented event, curatorial interpretation and commemorative message.

## Recap và bàn giao

1954–1975 cannot be reduced to one binary story. Provisional division produced two state-building projects; insurgency had both southern roots and northern support; United States intervention internationalized and escalated conflict; communist side depended on large external aid networks as well; South Vietnam had institutions and social constituencies of its own while also relying heavily on US support.

The war ended militarily in 1975 and state reunification followed in 1976. But ending war did not remove economic destruction, migration, regional distrust or international isolation.
