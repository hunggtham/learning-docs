# Historiography, Collective Memory & Heritage: lịch sử được tạo thành knowledge như thế nào?

## “Quá khứ” và “lịch sử” không hoàn toàn giống nhau

The past is everything that happened. History is a reconstruction built from surviving evidence, questions and interpretation.

This distinction matters because evidence is incomplete and uneven. Court leaves text; poor household may leave few documents. War destroys archives. Later regime selects what to preserve.

So historical method asks two questions at once:

1. source says what?
2. why does this source exist while other voices are absent?

## Primary source is not automatically truth

A royal chronicle, tax record, police file, memoir, newspaper and oral testimony are all primary sources in appropriate context, but each has production bias.

Court chronicle sees from court; colonial report sees through administrative categories; memoir sees through memory; propaganda material has persuasion goal.

Bias does not make source useless. Bias itself can be evidence about institution and worldview.

## Archaeology fills gaps—but also requires inference

Pottery, burial, wall, pollen, coin and weapon can reveal life where text is absent. But artifact does not “speak” on its own.

Archaeologist infers date/function/context from stratigraphy, comparison and scientific dating.

Good history distinguishes:

**observation → inference → interpretation**.

## Đại Việt sử ký and dynastic chronicles

Premodern Vietnamese chronicles preserve indispensable information on court, war, diplomacy and disaster. Yet they were compiled by officials within particular political/moral frameworks.

When a chronicle praises/condemns ruler, separate factual event from moral judgment.

Chronicle periodization also privileges dynastic centre; upland, village and minority community may appear only when interacting with state.

## Chinese sources: external but indispensable

For early periods, Chinese dynastic histories and administrative sources are major written evidence. They can preserve dates/names unavailable elsewhere but describe region through imperial categories.

Neither reject them as “foreign” nor accept them literally. Cross-check archaeology and other texts.

## Champa/Khmer inscriptions

Sanskrit, Cham and Khmer inscriptions help recover polities that a Đại Việt-centred narrative might reduce to opponents/frontiers.

Reading history of current Vietnam territory requires multilingual source base.

This is one reason library avoids treating modern national border as timeless container.

## Colonial archive

French administration generated huge record: census, tax, map, police, school, court, infrastructure and economic report.

Administrative abundance creates temptation to over-trust colonial categories. A category designed for governance can simplify fluid identities or informal economy.

Archive is powerful because state observed extensively, not because observation was neutral.

## Newspaper and public sphere

Twentieth-century newspapers let historians study debate, advertisement, language change and urban life.

But censorship, ownership and audience shape content.

A newspaper silence is not proof event absent; perhaps reporting was prohibited or rural event never reached editor.

## Oral history

Oral testimony is crucial for war, migration and everyday life. Memory can contain chronology errors while accurately preserving emotion, social relation and perceived causality.

Do not use “memory is imperfect” to discard testimony. Instead ask what type of claim it can support.

## National history and nation-building

Modern states use school textbook, museum, monument and anniversary to build shared narrative. Vietnam is no exception; neither are Korea, France, China or United States.

National narrative selects origin, hero, sacrifice and turning point to create collective identity.

Historical scholarship may overlap with national narrative but has different obligation: evidence, uncertainty and competing interpretation must remain visible.

## Heroic memory versus structural history

A battle is easier to remember through named hero than through logistics and fiscal system. Public memory therefore often personalizes structural process.

The library keeps hero story but adds mechanism:

- Ngô Quyền + tide/logistics;
- Trần commanders + supply strategy;
- Lê Lợi + coalition/reconstruction;
- Quang Trung + mobilization/operational speed.

This does not diminish individuals; it prevents explanation from becoming magic-person theory.

## Hùng Vương: myth, memory and archaeology

Hùng traditions are central identity memory. Archaeology demonstrates complex societies in relevant broad region/period but does not verify literal 18-kings chronology.

Both statements can coexist.

Memory can be historically important even when not literal archive.

## Contested twentieth-century terminology

Terms for 1945–1975 can encode perspective: “kháng chiến chống Mỹ”, “Vietnam War”, “Second Indochina War”, “civil war”, “national liberation war” each foregrounds different relationship.

Rather than select one label as complete explanation, scholarship should specify analytical layer: decolonization, interstate conflict, civil conflict, revolutionary war and Global Cold War overlapped.

Terminology must not silently replace argument.

## 1975 and different memories

1975 can be remembered as reunification/liberation by some communities and as defeat/displacement/exile by others, especially across diaspora histories.

Historical fact of state/military outcome is distinct from emotional/community meaning.

A mature public history can document multiple lived memories without claiming all factual interpretations have equal evidence.

## Museum as narrative technology

Museum does not merely store objects. Selection, label, room order and lighting build causal story.

When visiting museum, ask:

- who owns institution?
- when exhibition designed?
- what object gets prominence?
- what period/community is absent?
- which source supports label?

This makes museum visit active historical reading.

## Monument and site layers

A temple commemorating ancient figure may be rebuilt in Nguyễn/modern period. A war monument may be erected decades after event.

Therefore always separate:

```text
date of remembered event
≠
date of cult/monument origin
≠
date of present structure
```

This three-date model prevents major heritage misunderstanding.

## UNESCO inscription does not prove every narrative

UNESCO status usually recognizes cultural/historical value and preservation criteria. It should not be cited as proof for all local legends attached to site.

For Cổ Loa, archaeology supports ancient political centre; later temples preserve memory layer. For Hội An/Huế/Mỹ Sơn, current heritage landscape includes restoration and later use.

## Digital history

Digitized archive, GIS, satellite image and searchable text change research capability. Historians can compare place/time at new scale.

But digital availability introduces selection bias: what has been scanned becomes easier to cite than equally important undigitized material.

Search result ranking can shape historiography subtly.

## Source triangulation

For a disputed question, use different source types:

```text
textual record
+ material/archaeological evidence
+ external source
+ local/oral evidence
        ↓
stronger reconstruction
```

Sources need not “vote” equally; assess provenance and relevance.

## Confidence language

Library uses levels implicitly:

- **well established**: multiple independent evidence;
- **probable/plausible**: strong inference with gaps;
- **contested**: credible scholarly disagreement;
- **traditional/legendary**: important memory but not verified literally.

This is better than either overconfidence or saying “nobody knows anything”.

## Recap và bàn giao

Historiography is methodology for turning surviving evidence into accountable claims. Collective memory explains why societies remember selective past; heritage materializes that memory in landscape.
