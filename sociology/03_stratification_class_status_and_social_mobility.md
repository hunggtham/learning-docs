# Stratification, Class, Status & Social Mobility — Cách inequality được tạo, truyền và biến đổi

Social stratification là patterned inequality giữa social positions/groups về income, wealth, authority, prestige, security và opportunities. Sociology không chỉ hỏi “ai có nhiều hơn”, mà hỏi institutions nào phân bổ resources, boundaries nào hạn chế mobility, và advantages/disadvantages tích lũy qua life course như thế nào.

## 1. Inequality vs stratification

Inequality có thể là bất kỳ difference về resources/outcomes.

Stratification nhấn mạnh difference được tổ chức thành tương đối durable hierarchy gắn với social categories và institutions.

## 2. Income vs wealth

Income là flow; wealth là stock of assets minus liabilities.

Wealth thường bất bình đẳng hơn income và truyền intergenerational advantage qua housing, education, business capital và buffer against shocks.

## 3. Class

Class can be defined through income, occupation, employment relation, ownership/control of productive assets or market situation.

Different class schemes answer different questions; do not treat “class” as one self-evident variable.

## 4. Status

Status refers prestige/social honor. High income and high status often correlate but not perfectly.

Academic, religious or artistic positions can carry status disproportionate to income.

## 5. Power

Power is capacity to shape outcomes even with resistance, or to set agendas/rules defining choices.

Economic resources, organizational position, expertise and network centrality can all generate power.

## 6. Socioeconomic status

SES indexes combine education, occupation and income for practical analysis.

Useful descriptively, but aggregation can obscure different mechanisms of each component.

## 7. Absolute and relative mobility

Absolute mobility asks whether children have higher/lower class/income than parents in absolute terms.

Relative mobility asks how strongly origins predict destinations compared with others.

A growing economy can produce high absolute upward mobility while relative mobility remains low.

## 8. Intergenerational elasticity

Income persistence can be summarized by elasticity/correlation between parent and child economic outcomes.

Measurement requires long-run income and careful lifecycle timing; single-year income is noisy.

## 9. Mobility tables

Transition matrices show movement between origin and destination classes/quantiles.

Observed mobility combines structural change in occupational shares with exchange mobility.

## 10. Equality of opportunity

Conceptually distinguishes circumstances outside individual control from effort/choices.

Operationalization is normative and difficult because “effort” itself is shaped by circumstances.

## 11. Cumulative advantage

Initial advantage can compound through better schools, networks, confidence, credentials, health and wealth.

Small early differences can widen without one discriminatory event at every stage.

## 12. Matthew effect

Recognition/resources often flow toward already recognized actors, reinforcing advantage.

Seen in science, careers and cultural markets.

Need distinguish cumulative advantage from true productivity differences.

## 13. Social reproduction

Families transmit economic, cultural and social resources across generations.

Transmission mechanisms include wealth, neighborhood, school access, language practices, networks and expectations.

## 14. Cultural capital and education

Schools may reward communication styles, knowledge and dispositions more common in advantaged families.

This can reproduce inequality even under formally equal rules.

## 15. Social capital and networks

Job information/referrals often travel through networks. Network segregation can reproduce unequal opportunity.

Referrals may improve match quality while also excluding outsiders.

## 16. Credentialism

Credentials can signal skill, standardize selection and also serve as closure mechanisms restricting access to occupations.

Need distinguish genuine skill requirement from status competition.

## 17. Opportunity hoarding

Groups may monopolize valuable opportunities through licensing, neighborhood restrictions, legacy admissions, internal networks or organizational rules.

Mechanism requires identifiable boundary and control over resource.

## 18. Closure

Social closure restricts access to resources/status through categories or credentials.

Closure may protect quality/safety in some contexts and create rent/exclusion in others.

## 19. Labor-market segmentation

Labor markets can contain primary jobs with stability/careers and secondary jobs with low security/mobility.

Workers may face barriers moving between segments.

## 20. Precarious employment

Temporary, gig, informal and unstable work can shift risk from organization to worker.

Precarity depends not only contract type but predictability, bargaining power and social protection.

## 21. Occupational prestige

Occupations differ in status beyond pay.

Prestige rankings can shape aspirations and social interaction; cross-cultural/time comparison requires caution.

## 22. Spatial inequality

Neighborhoods differ in schools, transport, pollution, safety, jobs and networks.

Residential sorting makes place both outcome and cause of inequality.

## 23. Segregation

Segregation measures uneven distribution of groups across neighborhoods/schools/workplaces.

It can result from discrimination, preferences, income inequality, housing policy and network effects.

## 24. Neighborhood effects

Living in disadvantaged/advantaged area may affect outcomes through peers, schools, safety, pollution and networks.

Selection into neighborhoods is a major identification challenge.

## 25. Education expansion

Expanding education can increase average attainment while relative advantage persists through institution quality, field of study or postgraduate credentials.

This is credential inflation/qualitative differentiation rather than simple equalization.

## 26. Health inequality

Health follows socioeconomic gradients through material resources, stress, work conditions, environment, behavior and healthcare access.

Reverse causality also matters: poor health reduces earnings/employment.

## 27. Status stress

Relative position and perceived control can affect stress and health.

Evidence needs separate status mechanisms from material deprivation.

## 28. Gender stratification

Gender inequality can arise through occupational sorting, care responsibilities, organizational routines, bargaining, norms and discrimination.

Raw gaps should be decomposed cautiously; controls may themselves be outcomes of gendered institutions.

## 29. Race and ethnicity as social stratification categories

Race/ethnicity classifications are historically and institutionally constructed and can shape discrimination, residential patterns, networks and resource access.

Categories vary across countries/time; avoid treating them as fixed biological mechanisms.

## 30. Intersectionality

Social positions intersect: class, gender, ethnicity, migration status and age can produce experiences not reducible to additive independent effects.

Empirically, interaction and institutional context matter; intersectionality is more than listing identities.

## 31. Migration and stratification

Migrants may face credential nonrecognition, language barriers, legal status constraints and network segmentation.

Second-generation outcomes depend education, neighborhoods and discrimination as well as parental capital.

## 32. Elite reproduction

Elite positions can reproduce through selective schools, internships, networks, inheritance and cultural signals.

Formal meritocratic selection can coexist with unequal access to preparation.

## 33. Meritocracy

Meritocratic institutions reward measured achievement, but what counts as merit and access to skill-building are socially organized.

Sociological analysis distinguishes merit ideal, measurement and opportunity structure.

## 34. Poverty

Poverty can be absolute, relative, episodic or persistent.

Dynamics matter: households may cycle around threshold due shocks.

Poverty is not identical to low class status or social exclusion, though related.

## 35. Social exclusion

Exclusion concerns inability to participate in valued institutions/relations, not only income.

It can involve housing, digital access, documentation, transport, stigma or political/civic barriers.

## 36. Welfare institutions

Taxes/transfers/services reshape market-generated inequality and risks.

Comparative welfare regimes differ in decommodification, family roles, targeting/universality and labor-market institutions.

Detailed tax/economic incidence belongs in Economics/Public Economics.

## 37. Inequality and social cohesion

High inequality may affect trust, residential segregation, politics and health, but causality runs both directions and institutions mediate.

Avoid broad claims from country-level correlations alone.

## 38. Measurement

Use income/wealth distributions, mobility panels, occupations, education, neighborhoods, network data and qualitative life histories.

Different measures capture different dimensions; one inequality index is not complete social stratification.

## 39. Failure modes

Sai lầm thứ nhất là use income as synonym for class/wealth/status.

Sai lầm thứ hai là equate high absolute mobility with equal opportunity.

Sai lầm thứ ba là call every persistent advantage discrimination without tracing mechanism.

Sai lầm thứ tư là “control away” institutional pathways and call residual the only inequality.

Sai lầm thứ năm là treat social categories as timeless/universal.

## 40. Analysis template

1. dimension: income, wealth, status, authority hay security?
2. position/category được defined thế nào?
3. institution nào allocates resource?
4. family/network/place transmission mechanism nào?
5. barriers/closure ở transition nào?
6. advantage accumulates across life course ra sao?
7. absolute vs relative mobility khác thế nào?
8. spatial/organizational sorting có matter không?
9. category intersection/context nào?
10. evidence descriptive hay identifies causal mechanism?

Stratification is produced through institutions. Chapter tiếp theo studies those institutions—family, education, work, religion, media and political/civic organization—as interconnected systems rather than isolated topics.
