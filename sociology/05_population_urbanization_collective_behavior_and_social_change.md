# Population, Urbanization, Collective Behavior & Social Change — Demography, cities, movements và transformation

Societies change when population structures, migration, technology, institutions and collective action interact. Sociology studies not only whether population grows or cities expand, but how demographic composition changes relationships, institutions and inequality; how urban space concentrates opportunity and disadvantage; and how collective actors transform norms/rules.

## 1. Demography as social structure

Population is organized by age, sex/gender, household form, migration status, education and geography.

Changes in composition can alter schools, labor markets, housing, care systems and politics even if individual behavior stays constant.

## 2. Fertility

Fertility rates reflect family preferences plus education, childcare, housing, work, norms, health and policy.

Avoid monocausal cultural explanations when institutional constraints differ.

## 3. Mortality

Mortality varies by income, occupation, environment, gender, region and health systems.

Life expectancy is population summary and can hide within-group inequality.

## 4. Migration

Migration reallocates people across places and networks.

Push/pull language is only a start; legal status, networks, costs, information and household strategy matter.

## 5. Chain migration and networks

Previous migrants reduce information/housing/job-search costs for later migrants.

Networks can make flows persistent after original economic gap changes.

## 6. Assimilation and incorporation

Migrants may converge on language, education, occupation and identity at different rates.

Incorporation depends institutions and destination context, not one linear assimilation path.

## 7. Transnationalism

Migrants can maintain economic, family and cultural ties across borders through remittances, communication and travel.

Migration does not imply complete social detachment from origin.

## 8. Age structure

Population aging changes dependency, care demand, labor supply and intergenerational relations.

Ageing is demographic fact; its social effect depends retirement, migration, family and welfare institutions.

## 9. Cohorts

Birth cohorts share historical exposures at similar life stages.

Observed age differences can reflect aging, period shocks or cohort experience; separate them conceptually.

## 10. Household transformation

Later marriage, lower fertility, divorce, single-person households and longer lifespan reshape housing/care/social isolation patterns.

Household statistics should not be read as direct measures of loneliness or family breakdown.

## 11. Urbanization

Urbanization concentrates population in towns/cities and changes density, specialization, anonymity and social networks.

Cities create both agglomeration opportunities and congestion/exclusion.

## 12. Urban ecology

Neighborhood patterns emerge from land markets, transport, institutions, discrimination and household sorting.

Spatial pattern is not purely cultural preference.

## 13. Segregation

Residential segregation can reproduce school, safety, pollution and network inequalities.

Measure spatial distribution explicitly; citywide diversity can coexist with neighborhood segregation.

## 14. Gentrification

Investment/in-migration can raise amenities/property values while displacing or economically pressuring incumbent residents.

Need distinguish direct displacement, exclusion of future low-income residents and cultural/political displacement.

## 15. Housing as social infrastructure

Housing determines location, wealth, stability, school access and neighborhood ties.

Tenure systems and zoning shape stratification beyond shelter consumption.

## 16. Public space

Parks, transit, streets and commercial spaces structure encounter and inclusion.

Rules/security/design can make public spaces accessible to some groups more than others.

## 17. Rural sociology

Rural areas are shaped by agriculture, resource extraction, aging, service access and outmigration.

Do not treat rural as merely “not urban”; institutional/economic structures differ.

## 18. Collective behavior

Collective behavior includes crowds, rumors, panics, fads and emergent coordinated action outside established routines.

Modern research avoids assuming crowds are inherently irrational.

## 19. Social movements

Movements pursue/change collective claims through networks, organizations, identities and repertoires.

Movement emergence requires more than grievance—resources, opportunities, framing and networks matter.

## 20. Resource mobilization

Movements need money, skills, leadership, organizations and communication channels.

Widespread dissatisfaction alone does not create sustained movement.

## 21. Political opportunity

Institutional openness, elite divisions, repression and allies can change movement strategy/likelihood.

This is descriptive analytic framing, not guidance for influencing political outcomes.

## 22. Framing processes

Movements define problems, responsibility and action through frames.

Frame resonance depends cultural context and audience networks.

## 23. Repertoires of contention

Groups draw from historically available forms of collective action such as petitions, strikes, demonstrations or boycotts.

Repertoires evolve with technology/institutions.

## 24. Diffusion of protest/innovation

Collective action can spread through networks and observation.

Need separate common shocks from social contagion.

## 25. Countermovements

Institutional change can mobilize groups seeking to resist/reverse it.

Social change is relational conflict, not one-direction progression.

## 26. Collective efficacy

Communities can coordinate informal social control when trust/cohesion combine with willingness to act.

Cohesion alone is not sufficient if residents lack resources or shared expectations.

## 27. Technology and social change

Technology changes communication, surveillance, work, family coordination and organization scale.

Social institutions shape adoption/use; technological determinism is insufficient.

## 28. Digital networks

Online networks reduce communication cost and enable large-scale weak-tie coordination.

They also amplify algorithmic sorting, misinformation and visibility inequalities.

## 29. Globalization

Globalization connects migration, production, culture, organizations and institutions across borders.

It creates convergence in some practices and hybrid/local differentiation in others.

## 30. World-systems perspective

Global division of labor can be analyzed through unequal positions in production/trade networks.

Useful as structural lens but should not replace firm-level/institutional evidence or assume countries occupy immutable categories.

## 31. Modernization theory

Modernization theories link industrialization/urbanization/education to institutional/cultural change.

Historical diversity and path dependence challenge simple linear sequence toward one endpoint.

## 32. Dependency perspectives

Dependency approaches emphasize how external economic relationships can constrain domestic development.

Specific mechanisms—terms of trade, debt, ownership, technology control—should be identified rather than relying on label alone.

## 33. Social change through institutions

Major change often combines demographic/economic pressure with organizations, legal reform, movement activity and norm change.

One trigger rarely explains durable transformation.

## 34. Diffusion vs independent adaptation

Similar reforms across countries may spread through imitation/professional networks or emerge independently under common pressures.

Comparative design should distinguish both.

## 35. Path dependence

Earlier institutions/infrastructure can shape later options through sunk costs, networks and organizations.

Persistence alone is not path dependence; specify increasing returns/lock-in mechanism.

## 36. Social resilience

Communities/institutions respond to disasters, recessions or demographic shocks through resources, networks and governance.

Resilience should not romanticize adaptation that shifts burden to vulnerable groups.

## 37. Environmental sociology

Environmental risks are socially distributed and mediated by production, regulation, housing and inequality.

Detailed physical mechanisms belong in environmental science; sociology focuses exposure, institutions and collective response.

## 38. Risk society

Modern societies generate manufactured risks through technology/complex systems and depend expert institutions to assess them.

Risk perception and actual hazard should be analytically separated.

## 39. Measurement and methods

Population/social change uses censuses, surveys, GIS, administrative data, historical records, network data, ethnography and comparative cases.

Generic design/method details live in Research Methods.

## 40. Failure modes

Sai lầm thứ nhất là use demographic trend as direct causal explanation without institution mechanism.

Sai lầm thứ hai là treat cities as homogeneous units and ignore neighborhood sorting.

Sai lầm thứ ba là explain social movements by grievance alone.

Sai lầm thứ tư là technological determinism.

Sai lầm thứ năm là tell social change as one linear modernization path.

## 41. Analysis template

1. demographic composition nào changed?
2. institution/market absorbs change how?
3. spatial distribution matters how?
4. networks/migration create feedback nào?
5. organizations/resources enable collective action how?
6. frames/norms legitimate change or resistance how?
7. technology interacts with institutions how?
8. global vs local mechanisms differ where?
9. path dependence/critical events nào?
10. evidence separates common shock, selection and diffusion không?

With this chapter, Sociology core spans social structure → culture/identity/deviance → groups/networks/organizations → stratification → institutions → population/social change. The README and audit tie these together and define boundaries with Psychology, Economics, History and Research Methods.
