# Groups, Networks, Organizations & Bureaucracy — Relational structure và organized action

Social outcomes phụ thuộc không chỉ individual attributes mà còn positions in groups/networks và rules of organizations. Sociology of organization asks how coordination, hierarchy, routines and informal relations shape behavior; network sociology asks how ties create access, influence and constraint.

## 1. Social group

A group involves sustained interaction, shared identity or recognized membership. A statistical category like “people age 30–39” is not automatically a group.

## 2. Primary and secondary groups

Primary groups involve intimate durable relations; secondary groups are more task/role oriented.

Modern life mixes both: workplaces can create friendships; families also coordinate tasks/resources.

## 3. In-group and out-group

Group boundaries shape trust, solidarity and stereotypes. In-group favoritism can arise without explicit hostility toward outsiders.

## 4. Reference groups

People evaluate themselves relative to groups they belong to or aspire to join.

Reference groups influence consumption, career expectations and identity.

## 5. Group size

As groups grow, communication/coordination costs rise and direct mutual monitoring weakens.

Larger groups can mobilize more resources but face free-riding.

## 6. Dyads and triads

A dyad depends directly on two members and dissolves if one leaves. A triad introduces coalition, mediation and majority possibilities.

Adding one actor can qualitatively change relational structure.

## 7. Social networks

A network consists nodes and ties. Nodes may be people, firms, organizations or countries.

Network questions focus on relational position, not only node attributes.

## 8. Degree and centrality

Degree counts ties; centrality concepts ask different forms of structural importance.

High degree, brokerage and closeness are distinct advantages and should not be collapsed into “influence”.

## 9. Strong and weak ties

Strong ties provide trust/support; weak ties can connect actors to nonredundant information and opportunities.

Weak ties are not always better; function depends on task and network structure.

## 10. Structural holes

A broker connecting otherwise disconnected groups can access diverse information and control coordination.

Brokerage can create advantage but also trust burden.

## 11. Closure

Dense networks facilitate trust, norms and monitoring.

Closure and brokerage can be complementary: one supports cooperation, the other novelty/access.

## 12. Homophily

Ties form disproportionately among similar actors due preferences, opportunity structures and institutional sorting.

Homophily contributes to segregation and unequal information access.

## 13. Diffusion

Ideas, behaviors and innovations spread through networks.

Observed clustering can reflect diffusion or homophilous selection; causal identification needs designs separating them.

## 14. Threshold models

Individuals may adopt behavior when enough contacts adopt.

Different thresholds can generate cascades or stable nonadoption even with similar preferences.

## 15. Social capital

Social capital refers resources accessible through relationships—information, trust, support, obligations.

It can benefit insiders while excluding outsiders, so it is not uniformly socially beneficial.

## 16. Collective action

Groups need coordinate contributions to shared goals.

Selective incentives, identity, monitoring and leadership can overcome free-riding.

## 17. Organizations

Organizations are formalized collectives with roles, boundaries, goals and procedures.

They solve coordination problems but create hierarchy, agency and inertia.

## 18. Bureaucracy

Bureaucracy coordinates large-scale activity through specialization, hierarchy, written rules, records and offices.

Its strengths—predictability and impersonality—can also create rigidity and goal displacement.

## 19. Formal vs informal organization

Official charts describe formal authority; real work often relies on informal networks, norms and shortcuts.

Ignoring informal organization misrepresents implementation.

## 20. Routines

Routines store organizational memory and reduce decision costs.

They can improve reliability but persist after environment changes.

## 21. Organizational culture

Organizations develop shared assumptions, language and rituals.

“Culture” should be linked to recruitment, incentives, leadership and routines rather than treated as vague atmosphere.

## 22. Goal displacement

Measures/procedures created to serve goals can become goals themselves.

Examples include optimizing metrics rather than underlying service quality.

## 23. Goodhart-style mechanism

When a measure becomes target, actors can game it or shift effort away from unmeasured dimensions.

This links organizational sociology to principal-agent economics.

## 24. Institutional isomorphism

Organizations may become similar due coercive rules, professional norms and imitation under uncertainty.

Similarity therefore does not always prove one structure is technically optimal.

## 25. Organizational fields

Firms, regulators, professions, suppliers and associations interact within fields that define legitimate practices.

Field-level pressures shape organizations beyond internal efficiency.

## 26. Legitimacy

Organizations seek social acceptance as well as technical performance.

Adopting policies can be ceremonial if formal structures decouple from actual practice.

## 27. Decoupling

An organization may maintain official policy for legitimacy while everyday routines differ.

Research should compare formal documents with implementation evidence.

## 28. Professionalization

Professions control expertise through credentials, associations and ethical standards.

This can improve quality and also create closure/entry barriers.

## 29. Organizational ecology

Population ecology studies founding, survival and failure across organizational populations.

Density can increase legitimacy early and competition later.

## 30. Inertia

Large organizations may change slowly due sunk routines, politics, regulation and reliability requirements.

Inertia can be failure or valuable stability depending environment.

## 31. Power in organizations

Power comes from formal authority, expertise, control of resources/information, network brokerage and alternatives.

Titles are only one source.

## 32. Principal-agent relations

Organizations delegate tasks where goals/information differ.

Monitoring, incentives and professional norms are alternative governance mechanisms.

## 33. Street-level bureaucracy

Frontline workers translate policy into practice under limited resources and ambiguous rules.

Discretion means implementation can differ from formal policy.

## 34. Organizations and inequality

Hiring, promotion, scheduling and evaluation routines can generate inequality even without explicit discriminatory intent.

Opportunity structures are organizational, not only individual.

## 35. Remote/hybrid organization

Communication technology changes visibility, coordination and network formation.

Effects depend task interdependence, onboarding, documentation and informal knowledge flows.

## 36. Platform organizations

Platforms coordinate participants through algorithms, ratings and rules rather than classic hierarchy alone.

Algorithmic governance can concentrate rule-setting power while decentralizing task execution.

## 37. Network measurement

Network data can come from surveys, communication logs, affiliations or observed interaction.

Missing ties and boundary definition strongly affect metrics.

## 38. Failure modes

Sai lầm thứ nhất là reduce network effects to number of friends.

Sai lầm thứ hai là infer influence from similarity without handling homophily.

Sai lầm thứ ba là describe formal org chart as actual workflow.

Sai lầm thứ tư là treat bureaucracy as inherently bad rather than coordination form with trade-offs.

Sai lầm thứ năm là call every shared workplace behavior “culture” without transmission/enforcement mechanism.

## 39. Analysis template

1. group/network boundary là gì?
2. ties/resources flow theo đường nào?
3. centrality/closure/brokerage mechanism nào matter?
4. formal and informal rules differ where?
5. routines/metrics shape behavior how?
6. authority/resource/information power ở đâu?
7. legitimacy pressures vs technical efficiency thế nào?
8. homophily vs influence thế nào?
9. organization reproduces inequality by which routine?
10. evidence observes stated structure or actual interaction?

Groups and organizations distribute opportunities. Chapter tiếp theo focuses directly on stratification: class, status, mobility and cumulative advantage.
