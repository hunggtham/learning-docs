# Doanh nghiệp nước ngoài và FDI tại Hàn Quốc (Foreign-Invested Companies / 외국인투자기업)

Kinh tế Hàn Quốc không chỉ gồm Korean firms đi ra thế giới. Multinationals cũng vào Korea để bán hàng, đặt R&D, manufacturing, logistics, finance, software hoặc strategic partnership. Nhưng cụm “công ty nước ngoài tại Hàn” che giấu nhiều architecture khác nhau: **subsidiary, branch, liaison office, joint venture, acquisition và greenfield investment**.

Legal architecture ảnh hưởng liability, tax, hiring, transfer pricing, governance và decision speed. Vì vậy trước khi hỏi “công ty này của Mỹ/Nhật/Châu Âu ở Korea thế nào?”, cần xác định exact entity structure.

> Mental model: foreign market entry là bài toán **legal node + capital flow + decision rights + localization + ecosystem fit**.

## Foreign-invested company khác foreign branch

A **foreign-invested company / 외국인투자기업** can be a Korean domestic corporation with foreign investor ownership.

A **branch / 지점** is generally an extension of the foreign corporation rather than a separate Korean incorporated entity.

A **liaison office / 연락사무소** performs limited non-revenue activities within permitted scope, such as market research/contact.

The simple mental model:

```text
Subsidiary = new legal node in Korea
Branch     = arm of foreign legal node
Liaison    = limited non-revenue presence
```

This difference is not cosmetic paperwork. It changes risk ring-fencing and governance.

## Limited liability and ring-fencing

A Korean subsidiary normally has own equity, board, contracts and liabilities.

If subsidiary fails, parent exposure depends on invested capital, guarantees and contractual support rather than automatic unlimited liability.

Branch liabilities are more directly tied to foreign corporation because branch is not same kind of separate legal entity.

Thus entity choice is also **risk architecture**.

## FDI classification: do not confuse investment recognition with minimum corporate capital

Invest KOREA describes a typical route under foreign-investment framework involving at least 100 million KRW investment and at least 10% of voting shares, with statutory details and exceptions depending on case.

Important distinction:

- FDI recognition criteria;
- corporate incorporation requirements;
- business-license requirements;
- visa/employment requirements

are not the same question.

Do not interpret an FDI threshold as universal minimum capital needed for every foreign-related corporation.

Current transaction should always be checked under current Foreign Investment Promotion Act and relevant guidance.

## Entry mode 1: Greenfield investment

**Greenfield investment / 그린필드 투자** means building a new operation rather than buying existing company.

Advantages:

- full design control;
- new technology/process;
- clean organizational structure;
- location optimized for current strategy.

Costs:

- slow time-to-market;
- permits/construction;
- hiring from zero;
- supplier qualification;
- customer trust must be built.

Greenfield is common logic for factories, R&D centers, data centers and distribution sites where physical/network architecture matters.

## Entry mode 2: Acquisition

Buying an existing Korean company gives immediate access to:

- customers;
- employees;
- permits;
- local brand;
- distribution;
- supplier relationships.

But acquisition adds **integration risk**.

Potential friction:

- culture;
- ERP/process;
- compensation;
- customer retention;
- legacy liabilities;
- key-person departure.

Purchase price can destroy value if expected synergy is overestimated.

## Entry mode 3: Joint Venture

**Joint Venture / 합작회사** creates entity jointly owned by strategic partners.

JV works well when capabilities are complementary: one party has technology, other has market access, customer commitment, land, capital or manufacturing capability.

But ownership percentage alone does not solve governance.

Key provisions include:

- board composition;
- reserved matters;
- capital-call obligations;
- IP ownership;
- related-party pricing;
- deadlock mechanism;
- exit rights;
- non-compete boundaries.

A 50:50 JV without deadlock rules can be fragile.

## Strategic customer JV

Battery/manufacturing projects often use JV because supplier and customer both want commitment.

Customer obtains supply security; supplier obtains demand visibility; both share capital burden.

But customer concentration increases.

If OEM strategy changes, JV plant utilization can suffer despite signed strategic partnership.

JV reduces one type of uncertainty while creating another governance dependency.

## Market-seeking vs efficiency-seeking vs strategic-asset FDI

Not all FDI has same purpose.

### Market-seeking

Enter Korea to sell to Korean customers.

### Efficiency-seeking

Use Korea as manufacturing/logistics base when ecosystem productivity justifies cost.

### Strategic-asset seeking

Access engineers, technology, R&D partners, suppliers or advanced customers.

### Supply-chain positioning

Locate near Samsung, SK, Hyundai or bio/industrial clusters to qualify and co-develop.

Knowing purpose helps explain why company chooses Korea despite wage/land costs.

## Cluster pull: supplier follows anchor customer

Advanced manufacturing suppliers may locate near Korean fabs/auto plants because proximity reduces:

- qualification time;
- emergency response;
- logistics delay;
- engineering communication cost.

This is **customer-following FDI**.

Korea as market may be smaller than region, but being near major advanced-manufacturing customer can still justify local operation.

See [24_regional_clusters_and_industrial_geography](./24_regional_clusters_and_industrial_geography.md).

## Subsidiary autonomy: same brand, different job reality

Two Korean subsidiaries of global firms can have completely different authority.

Subsidiary A may only execute sales under APAC HQ decisions.

Subsidiary B may own local P&L, engineering and product decisions.

For employee and company analysis, ask:

- Who owns budget?
- Who sets price?
- Can local team hire independently?
- Where is product roadmap decided?
- Is Korean office revenue center, cost center or R&D center?
- Does local CEO report to regional or global HQ?

Logo tells little about decision rights.

## Matrix organization: local and global reporting collide

Multinational employee may report to Korean manager operationally and regional/global functional leader professionally.

This creates matrix benefits:

- global standards;
- local responsiveness;
- skill communities.

But also conflict when priorities differ.

Good matrix requires explicit decision rights. Otherwise employee spends time reconciling two bosses.

## Transfer pricing: price inside multinational group

Related entities trade services, components, IP, loans and management support.

**Transfer pricing / 이전가격** determines intra-group price.

Tax authorities generally expect arm’s-length principles under applicable rules.

For analyst, transfer pricing changes local reported margin.

Example:

```text
Korean subsidiary revenue: 100
Royalty/service paid to parent: 15
Local operating cost: 75
Local operating profit: 10
```

If royalty changes, local margin changes even with same customer economics.

Thus low Korean margin does not automatically mean local management inefficient.

Need understand **functional profile**: sales distributor, contract manufacturer, entrepreneur, IP owner?

## Intercompany loans and funding

Parent may fund subsidiary through:

- equity;
- intercompany loan;
- guarantee;
- external local borrowing.

Each has tax, interest, FX and leverage implications.

If subsidiary earns KRW but owes USD parent loan, currency mismatch can appear.

Funding architecture should match cash-flow currency and business risk.

## Permanent establishment and tax presence

A foreign company can create Korean tax obligations depending on activities and treaty/legal facts even without full subsidiary.

Physical office, dependent agents or other activities may matter depending on rules.

This is specialist tax/legal territory; operational teams should not assume “no 법인 = no tax presence”.

The conceptual lesson is that **economic presence and legal/tax presence are related but not identical**.

## Repatriation of profit

A profitable Korean subsidiary can return value to foreign parent through legitimate channels such as dividends or other arm’s-length transactions, subject to tax/legal rules.

But local retained earnings may also fund Korean expansion.

Parent capital-allocation choice depends on:

- local growth opportunity;
- tax;
- FX;
- capital controls/rules;
- group liquidity needs.

For employee, profitable subsidiary does not guarantee reinvestment locally if HQ allocates cash elsewhere.

## Localization: translation is only surface layer

True localization includes:

- product specification;
- pricing;
- payment method;
- distribution;
- customer support;
- regulation;
- enterprise procurement;
- marketing;
- HR;
- after-sales service;
- local decision speed.

A product can have perfect Korean UI but fail because business model does not fit Korean buying process.

Localization is **go-to-market redesign**, not language conversion.

## B2C localization vs B2B localization

Consumer company may need local brand/channel/influencer strategy.

B2B industrial/IT vendor may need:

- Korean technical support;
- local reference customer;
- security certification;
- procurement registration;
- integration with Korean systems;
- local service SLA.

Therefore localization requirement depends on buying unit.

## Foreign firm inside Korean conglomerate supply chain

Supplying Samsung/Hyundai/SK ecosystem can require quality certification, cost-down expectations, local response and long engineering cycles.

Once supplier qualifies, relationship can become sticky because switching cost high.

This can create moat but also customer concentration risk.

Local subsidiary may be economically dependent on one anchor customer even if global parent diversified.

## Incentives: support changes NPV, not business reality

Foreign investors may access national/local incentives depending on investment type, location and policy.

Economic effect is same as other industrial policy:

\[
NPV_{after\ incentive} > NPV_{before\ incentive}
\]

But incentive cannot create customers or operational capability.

A location chosen purely for subsidy may underperform if talent, logistics or power inadequate.

## Local content and value added

FDI amount alone does not tell domestic contribution.

A Korean plant can import most components and repatriate IP fees, creating less domestic value added than headline investment suggests.

Another foreign company may create strong local supplier/R&D network.

To assess economic contribution, ask:

- local payroll;
- local procurement;
- R&D;
- exports;
- taxes;
- supplier development;
- technology spillover.

Gross investment is only one dimension.

## Technology transfer and spillovers

Foreign firm can bring process knowledge, management practices and global standards.

Local workers/suppliers learn and may later transfer knowledge elsewhere.

This is **knowledge spillover / 지식 파급효과**.

But spillover is not automatic. If operation is isolated assembly with little local engineering, learning may be limited.

Absorptive capacity of local suppliers/workers matters.

## Hiring and compensation in foreign-invested companies

Foreign-invested firm may combine Korean labor law with global HR framework.

Potential characteristics:

- English titles;
- regional pay bands;
- matrix reporting;
- global performance system;
- local Korean benefits;
- HQ stock compensation in some cases.

English title does not imply flat hierarchy or HQ-level authority.

Career analysis should ask whether Korean role owns product/customer/business decisions or mainly executes HQ strategy.

## Cross-cultural management

HQ may favor explicit global process; Korean customers/team may rely on high-context relationship and fast local response.

Successful subsidiary creates bridge rather than choosing one side.

Good practice:

- global policy for risk/control;
- local autonomy where market knowledge matters;
- written decision rights;
- bilingual documentation;
- clear escalation path.

## Exit and restructuring

Foreign parent may sell subsidiary, close branch, merge with another entity or relocate production.

Local employees/suppliers therefore face **parent portfolio risk** independent of local profitability.

A Korean subsidiary can be profitable but sold because global parent exits product category.

This is important when evaluating job stability or customer concentration.

## How to analyze a foreign company in Korea

Use this sequence:

1. Exact legal entity: subsidiary, branch or JV?
2. Ownership and parent guarantee/support?
3. Korea function: sales, R&D, manufacturing, services?
4. Revenue source: Korean customers or export/intercompany?
5. Decision rights: Korea vs regional/global HQ?
6. Transfer-pricing/function profile?
7. Funding structure and FX exposure?
8. Local supplier/talent ecosystem?
9. Regulation/licensing?
10. Parent strategic commitment to Korea?

For employment analysis, add pay band, reporting language, promotion path and skill portability.

## Mental Model

> A foreign company in Korea is an **interface between two systems**: global corporate architecture and Korean market/institutional environment. Performance depends not only on product quality but on whether legal structure, decision rights, localization and local ecosystem fit together.

## Common misconceptions

Foreign-invested company incorporated in Korea is not simply a “foreign corporation” in legal sense.

FDI classification threshold is not universal minimum capital for all foreign business activity.

Global brand prestige does not guarantee Korean subsidiary has broad authority or strong P&L.

Localization is not translation.

High FDI amount does not automatically mean high Korean domestic value added.

JV 50:50 does not mean governance is automatically balanced; deadlock design matters.
