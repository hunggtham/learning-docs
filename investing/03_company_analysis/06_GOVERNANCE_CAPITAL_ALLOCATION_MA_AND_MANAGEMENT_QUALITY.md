# 06 — Governance, Capital Allocation, M&A và Management Quality

Một doanh nghiệp có business tốt vẫn có thể trở thành khoản đầu tư tệ nếu management phân bổ vốn kém, governance yếu hoặc incentives lệch với minority shareholders. Chapter này tập trung vào câu hỏi thường bị bỏ qua khi đọc báo cáo tài chính: **ai đang quyết định sử dụng capital, họ có động cơ gì, và value tạo ra ở business level có thực sự chảy tới mỗi cổ phần hay không?**

Management quality phải được đánh giá qua evidence kéo dài nhiều năm, không qua presentation skill hay optimistic guidance.

# Phần I — Management Quality

## 1. Operating skill và capital-allocation skill

CEO có thể vận hành tốt nhưng allocate capital tệ. Operating skill thể hiện ở pricing, execution, product, cost, hiring và operations. Capital-allocation skill thể hiện ở capex, M&A, debt, dividend, buyback và issuance.

Investor cần tách hai skill sets.

## 2. Strategy clarity

Good management có thể giải thích company creates value ở đâu, competitive advantage nào cần defend và capital sẽ được ưu tiên theo hierarchy nào.

Strategy quá rộng kiểu “grow globally through innovation” không đủ để evaluate.

## 3. Execution track record

Compare promises with outcomes over several years: revenue targets, margins, capex, product launches, restructuring and deleveraging.

One miss is not proof of bad management; repeated unexplained misses are information.

## 4. Response under stress

Management quality often becomes visible during downturn. Does team protect liquidity, communicate uncertainty, cut bad projects and preserve strategic assets?

Boom-time performance can hide poor discipline.

## 5. Candor

High-quality communication separates controllable vs uncontrollable drivers and acknowledges mistakes.

Constant blame on macro/FX while taking credit for all positives is governance signal.

# Phần II — Capital Allocation

## 6. Capital allocation hierarchy

After operations generate cash, choices include maintenance capex, growth capex, R&D, M&A, debt reduction, dividends, buybacks or cash retention.

Every won/dollar has opportunity cost.

## 7. Maintenance capex comes first

Before calling FCF “excess cash”, business must spend enough to preserve competitive capacity.

Under-maintenance boosts short-term FCF but can damage future economics.

## 8. Growth capex

Growth capex is attractive only if expected incremental return exceeds cost of capital with sufficient margin.

Revenue growth by itself does not prove value creation.

## 9. Incremental ROIC

Historical ROIC describes old capital. Future value depends more on return from new capital.

If mature assets earn 25% but new projects only 7%, growth can dilute quality.

## 10. Reinvestment rate

Growth can be framed approximately:

`Growth ≈ Reinvestment Rate × Return on Incremental Capital`

High reinvestment at low return destroys value; modest reinvestment at high return can compound strongly.

## 11. ROIC vs WACC

Economic value is created when sustainable ROIC exceeds cost of capital. The spread matters more than growth alone.

But WACC and ROIC are estimates; use ranges and through-cycle thinking.

## 12. Capital intensity

Asset-light businesses may grow with little incremental capital. Capital-heavy sectors require factories, inventories or networks.

Same revenue growth can create very different shareholder value.

## 13. Working-capital reinvestment

Growth may consume cash through receivables and inventory. Analysts focusing only capex can underestimate reinvestment needs.

Negative working-capital models can fund growth, but reversal is dangerous when sales slow.

## 14. R&D as investment

Accounting expenses R&D in many sectors, but economic value may extend years. Evaluate R&D productivity by pipeline, product success and gross profit generated.

Capitalizing R&D analytically can improve comparability but requires assumptions.

## 15. Marketing as investment

Customer-acquisition spend can also create multi-year value when retention is strong.

For subscription/business models, distinguish maintenance marketing from growth CAC conceptually.

# Phần III — Dividends

## 16. Dividend is capital allocation

Dividend returns cash when internal reinvestment opportunities are lower or shareholders value predictable distributions.

High payout is not inherently shareholder-friendly if balance sheet weak.

## 17. Payout ratio

Dividend/net income is common, but FCF payout may better reflect cash sustainability for non-financial firms.

Cyclical peak earnings can make payout appear low just before downturn.

## 18. Dividend yield traps

Very high yield can reflect falling share price and expected cut. Check coverage, debt maturities, cyclicality and policy.

Yield alone is not value.

## 19. Progressive vs variable dividends

Some companies target stable/growing dividend; commodity companies may use variable dividends tied to FCF.

Investor should understand policy rather than extrapolate one payment.

# Phần IV — Buybacks

## 20. Buyback creates value only under conditions

Buyback creates per-share value if shares are purchased below intrinsic value and better uses of capital are absent.

Repurchasing overpriced stock destroys value despite EPS accretion.

## 21. EPS accretion vs economic value

Reducing share count mechanically lifts EPS. But if company borrows at high cost to buy expensive shares, economic value can fall.

Never evaluate buyback solely from EPS.

## 22. Offset SBC

A company may report large repurchases while diluted share count barely falls because buyback offsets employee issuance.

Track net share count, not gross buyback dollars.

## 23. Cyclical buyback mistake

Companies often buy aggressively at cycle peaks when cash abundant and stop at troughs when stock cheap.

Countercyclical discipline is valuable but requires strong balance sheet.

## 24. Tender offers and accelerated repurchases

Different mechanisms affect timing and price. Large accelerated buyback concentrates execution risk if valuation wrong.

Read terms rather than treating all repurchases equally.

# Phần V — Share Issuance and Dilution

## 25. Equity issuance can be rational

Issuing shares at very high valuation to fund high-return project can create value even though dilution occurs.

Question is value received per share issued.

## 26. Dilution math

Ownership percentage falls when new shares issued unless investor participates. Per-share metrics matter more than total-company growth.

Track basic and diluted shares over years.

## 27. Stock-based compensation

SBC is real economic compensation. Cash-flow statement adds it back because non-cash, but shareholder dilution remains.

FCF ignoring dilution overstates shareholder economics.

## 28. Options and RSUs

Options depend strike and vesting; RSUs are shares subject vesting. Fully diluted count should include economically relevant instruments.

Read compensation note.

# Phần VI — Debt and Balance-Sheet Allocation

## 29. Debt repayment

Reducing debt can be high-return use of capital when borrowing costs high or refinancing risk elevated.

Lower leverage also increases strategic flexibility.

## 30. Fixed vs floating debt

Floating debt reprices quickly; fixed debt creates maturity/refinancing cliff later.

Capital allocation should account not only amount but maturity ladder.

## 31. Maturity wall

Large debt due in one period can force refinance under unfavorable market. Management that staggers maturities reduces funding risk.

## 32. Credit rating vs equity optimization

Management may choose lower leverage to preserve rating and supplier/customer confidence even if textbook WACC suggests more debt.

Strategic optionality has value.

## 33. Cash hoarding

Cash protects downside and funds opportunities, but persistent excess cash lowers capital efficiency.

Poor managers may turn cash pile into overpriced acquisitions.

## 34. Net cash is not automatically good governance

Ask why cash exists, currency/location restrictions, required operating liquidity and capital-return policy.

Idle cash with no plan can deserve discount.

# Phần VII — M&A

## 35. Why companies acquire

Reasons include product, geography, customer access, technology, capacity, consolidation or financial engineering.

“Strategic” must translate into cash-flow mechanism.

## 36. Purchase price

Value creation starts with price paid. Great target acquired at excessive price can be bad deal.

Buyer should compare standalone intrinsic value plus realizable synergies against total consideration and integration cost.

## 37. Premium paid

Acquisition premium over unaffected market price is not automatically overpayment; market price may undervalue target. But large premium increases required synergies.

Quantify rather than narrate.

## 38. Synergy

Cost synergies may be more controllable than revenue synergies. Revenue synergy depends customer behavior and cross-selling execution.

Model timing, one-off costs and probability.

## 39. Integration cost

Systems migration, employee retention, restructuring, facilities closure and cultural integration consume cash/time.

Synergy headline without integration cost is incomplete.

## 40. Goodwill

Goodwill arises when purchase price exceeds fair value of identifiable net assets.

Repeated goodwill impairments can signal acquisition overpayment, though impairment timing is accounting judgment.

## 41. Intangible assets

Acquisition may recognize customer relationships, brands, patents or technology and amortize them.

Adjusted earnings excluding amortization should still respect that acquired intangibles had economic cost.

## 42. Accretive deal trap

Debt-financed acquisition of lower-P/E target can be immediately EPS accretive even with weak economic return.

Accretion is arithmetic, not proof of value creation.

## 43. Organic vs acquired growth

`Reported Growth = Organic + Acquisition + FX + other effects`

Separate them. Serial acquirers can hide weak organic business behind deal flow.

## 44. Serial acquirers

Serial M&A can work if integration playbook, decentralized operations and valuation discipline are strong.

But it can also create complex accounting, debt and constant adjusted metrics.

## 45. Roll-up risk

Roll-ups use acquisitions to grow reported scale. Risks include overpayment, leverage, integration, accounting complexity and eventually insufficient targets.

Track organic growth and per-share FCF.

## 46. Divestitures

Selling non-core/low-return assets can improve capital allocation. Evaluate sale price, tax leakage and use of proceeds.

Shrinkage can create value.

## 47. Spin-offs

Spin-offs can improve focus/incentives and reveal hidden value but may leave debt or duplicated costs.

Standalone economics matter more than event narrative.

# Phần VIII — Governance Architecture

## 48. Board of Directors

Board hires/monitors management, approves major capital actions and represents shareholder interests.

Look beyond independence label to expertise, tenure, attendance, ownership and relationships.

## 49. Board committees

Audit, compensation and nomination committees handle specific oversight. Audit-committee quality is especially important for financial reporting and related-party controls.

## 50. Chair and CEO roles

Separate chair/CEO may improve oversight but structure alone doesn't guarantee governance. Lead independent director can partly compensate.

Judge evidence.

## 51. Controlling shareholder

Concentrated owner can provide long horizon and monitoring, but minority investors face expropriation risk.

Related-party deals, restructuring and voting power deserve scrutiny.

## 52. Pyramid and cross-holdings

Complex ownership structures can allow control with limited economic ownership and obscure cash transfers.

Map ownership rather than relying company name alone.

## 53. Dual-class shares

Dual-class grants unequal voting rights. Founder control can protect long-term investment but weaken accountability.

Voting and economic ownership should be compared.

## 54. Minority shareholder treatment

Historical treatment during mergers, tender offers, placements and related-party transactions is practical governance evidence.

Past behavior often more informative than governance statements.

# Phần IX — Related-Party Transactions

## 55. Related-party dealings

Transactions with affiliates are not automatically bad but require fair pricing and transparent approval.

Look for unusual loans, guarantees, asset sales, purchases or service contracts.

## 56. Tunneling

Tunneling transfers value from listed company/minority holders to controlling parties through non-arm's-length transactions.

Governance discount may be economically justified if risk persistent.

## 57. Guarantees and contingent liabilities

Company may guarantee affiliate debt. This creates off-balance-sheet-like economic exposure even before payment occurs.

Read notes.

# Phần X — Executive Incentives

## 58. Compensation design

Revenue-based targets encourage growth; EBITDA targets may ignore capital; EPS targets may encourage buybacks; ROIC/FCF/per-share metrics can align better but can also be gamed.

Metric design shapes behavior.

## 59. Short-term vs long-term incentives

Annual bonuses can encourage quarter optimization. Multi-year vesting and ownership requirements can extend horizon.

But long-term equity plans still depend grant design and dilution.

## 60. Relative TSR

Total shareholder return vs peer group can reduce market-beta reward, but peer selection can be manipulated.

Inspect methodology.

## 61. Clawbacks

Clawback provisions allow recovery of compensation after misconduct/restatement in some regimes. Their existence and enforceability differ.

## 62. Insider ownership

Meaningful economic ownership can align incentives, but excessive control can entrench management.

Balance alignment and accountability.

## 63. Insider transactions

Single sale may reflect taxes/diversification. Patterns across insiders and time are more informative.

Purchases with personal capital can signal confidence but are not guaranteed alpha.

# Phần XI — Communication and Disclosure

## 64. Guidance quality

Good guidance explains assumptions, range and sensitivities. Constantly changing KPI definitions reduce trust.

Track historical guidance accuracy.

## 65. KPI disappearance

When company stops reporting a KPI as it deteriorates, information quality may worsen.

Compare investor presentations across years.

## 66. Adjusted metrics

Non-GAAP/adjusted metrics can clarify recurring economics but repeated exclusion of recurring “one-offs” is red flag.

Reconcile to statutory statements.

## 67. Earnings-call language

Avoid sentiment-reading theatrics. Use transcripts to compare explanations, priorities and consistency.

What management avoids can be as informative as prepared remarks.

## 68. Capital-markets-day promises

Long-term margin/growth targets should be tracked against actual delivery. Repeated resets deserve lower confidence.

# Phần XII — Governance Red Flags

## 69. Frequent auditor changes

Auditor changes are not proof misconduct but warrant investigation, especially with accounting disputes or delayed filings.

## 70. CFO turnover

Repeated CFO changes can signal internal control or strategy issues. Context matters.

## 71. Late filings/restatements

Restatements indicate reporting errors and sometimes control weaknesses. Understand materiality and cause.

## 72. Related-party complexity

Large affiliate networks, circular transactions or opaque SPVs increase analysis burden and governance risk.

## 73. Persistent dilution

If company repeatedly issues stock despite claimed strong FCF, reconcile cash needs and compensation.

## 74. Acquisition-adjustment treadmill

If every acquisition creates “one-time” integration charges indefinitely, normalized earnings may be overstated.

## 75. Debt-funded shareholder returns

Borrowing to fund dividends/buybacks can be rational in limited cases but dangerous if leverage rises without durable cash flow.

# Phần XIII — Evaluating Capital Allocation Quantitatively

## 76. Per-share FCF growth

Total FCF growth should be compared with diluted share count. Shareholder economics live per share.

## 77. Incremental ROIC estimate

One rough approach compares change in NOPAT over multi-year period with cumulative net investment.

Noisy, but useful to detect deteriorating returns.

## 78. Acquisition return

Estimate acquired operating profit/FCF relative total purchase price plus assumed debt and integration cost.

Compare with buyer cost of capital.

## 79. Buyback yield

Net share reduction relative beginning shares is more informative than announced dollars.

Combine with valuation paid.

## 80. Capital-return yield

Dividend yield + net buyback yield approximates direct capital return, but debt-funded payouts and dilution must be accounted.

## 81. Debt reduction return

Paying down 7% debt gives roughly risk-adjusted benefit close to avoided interest before considering tax/optionality. Compare with uncertain reinvestment return.

# Phần XIV — Capital Allocation through the Cycle

## 82. Boom phase

Cash abundant and valuations high. Discipline means resisting empire building and peak-cycle capex.

## 83. Downturn

Strong balance-sheet management preserves liquidity and can buy assets/shares cheaply.

Companies forced to issue equity at trough reveal prior balance-sheet fragility.

## 84. Countercyclical capital allocation

Best allocator may invest when competitors retrench, but only if balance sheet survives stress.

Countercyclical is not same as reckless catching falling knives.

# Phần XV — Jurisdiction and Ownership Structure

## 85. Governance varies by market

Disclosure, shareholder rights, takeover rules and ownership concentration differ by jurisdiction. Apply local legal context.

Do not import US governance assumptions blindly into Korea, Vietnam or other markets.

## 86. Family-controlled groups

Family control can create long horizon and group synergies, but related-party and succession transactions require scrutiny.

Evaluate actual history of minority treatment.

## 87. State influence

State ownership or regulation may create strategic benefits and non-financial objectives. Profit maximization may not be sole objective.

Valuation should reflect this where material.

# Phần XVI — Management Quality Scorecard

## 88. Operating evidence

Track margins, customer retention, market share, productivity and execution against plans.

## 89. Capital evidence

Track incremental ROIC, per-share FCF, dilution, leverage, M&A returns and buyback timing.

## 90. Governance evidence

Track related-party behavior, board quality, disclosure, incentives and minority treatment.

## 91. Communication evidence

Track forecast accuracy, KPI consistency and willingness to acknowledge mistakes.

The scorecard should organize evidence, not produce false-precision numeric score.

# Phần XVII — Connecting Governance to Valuation

## 92. Governance discount

Weak governance can justify lower multiple because future cash may not accrue proportionally to minority holders or reinvestment may destroy value.

“Cheap” company may be cheap for reason.

## 93. Management premium

Excellent allocator can deserve premium when long reinvestment runway exists. But premium itself can become excessive.

Great management does not make any price attractive.

## 94. Terminal value and capital allocation

DCF terminal assumptions implicitly assume future reinvestment and returns. Governance quality affects credibility of those assumptions.

## 95. Per-share value is final test

A company can double revenue and enterprise value while per-share value stagnates because dilution/debt/M&A consume gains.

Always translate business success to shareholder-per-share economics.

## 96. Final due-diligence questions

Before investing, explain: who controls company; how capital is allocated; incremental returns; dilution history; M&A record; debt policy; incentive design; related parties; disclosure quality; and evidence minority holders participate fairly.

If these questions are unanswered, company analysis is incomplete.

## 97. Kết luận

Business quality answers whether company can create economic value. Capital allocation answers what management does with that value. Governance answers whether minority shareholders receive a fair share.

A complete investment thesis needs all three layers. Strong operations cannot permanently rescue destructive capital allocation, and attractive valuation cannot fully compensate for governance risk you do not understand.
