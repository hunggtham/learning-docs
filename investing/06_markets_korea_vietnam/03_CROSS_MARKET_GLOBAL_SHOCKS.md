# Cross-market playbook — cùng một shock, Korea và Vietnam phản ứng khác nhau

> Global shocks không truyền giống nhau sang mọi country. Korea có export/semiconductor/market-sensitive KRW channel lớn; Vietnam có banking/property/domestic-liquidity/managed-FX channels khác. File này dùng cùng một causal framework để tránh copy một khẩu quyết như “Fed tăng thì stock giảm” cho mọi market.

## 1. Framework chung

Mọi shock nên đi qua chuỗi:

`Shock → Global growth/inflation → Fed/global yields/USD → local FX/policy/credit → sector earnings → valuation → flows/liquidity → asset price`

Ở mỗi bước hỏi: direction, magnitude, timing và phần nào đã priced.

## 2. First-order vs second-order effects

First-order là tác động trực tiếp: oil spike làm fuel cost tăng. Second-order: inflation lên → central bank less dovish → financing cost/property demand weaker.

Second-order sometimes dominates. Investor chỉ nhìn direct beneficiary có thể bỏ qua policy response.

## 3. Demand shock vs supply shock

Same commodity/price move can reflect different shock. Strong global demand raises oil/copper and exports; supply disruption raises oil but hurts real income.

Distinguish before sector positioning.

## 4. Growth surprise up, inflation stable

This Goldilocks-like shock tends support cyclicals/earnings while yields may rise moderately. Korea exporters/semis can benefit via global demand. Vietnam exporters, industrial parks/logistics and domestic sentiment may improve.

If valuations already price strong recovery, upside can be limited.

## 5. Growth up, inflation up

Overheating raises earnings near term but policy/rates become restrictive. Long-duration growth valuations can compress.

Korea may see exporters strong but KRW/yields interaction mixed. Vietnam domestic liquidity/property can face rate/FX constraints.

## 6. Growth down, inflation down

Disinflationary slowdown usually brings easing expectations and lower yields. Long bonds may benefit; equities depend earnings severity.

Korea export cyclicals sensitive. Vietnam can use domestic policy/public investment if FX/inflation permit, but bank asset quality matters.

## 7. Growth down, inflation up

Stagflation is difficult: earnings pressure plus high discount rates. Energy importers suffer.

Korea has meaningful energy-import channel; Vietnam also faces inflation/fuel cost but some upstream energy companies may benefit.

## 8. Fed hawkish inflation shock

US inflation beat → expected Fed path up → 2Y/real yields/USD often up.

Korea: USD/KRW pressure, foreign equity de-risking, growth multiple compression; exporters receive some FX translation benefit but financing/input costs can rise.

Vietnam: USD/VND pressure reduces easing space; foreign flow/valuation headwind; leveraged property/brokers sensitive through liquidity.

## 9. Fed dovish soft landing

If Fed easing because inflation normalized while growth remains okay, lower discount rates can support global risk assets.

Korea: semis/exporters benefit if demand intact, KRW may strengthen. Vietnam: FX pressure eases, domestic rates/liquidity flexibility improves.

This is very different from emergency cuts.

## 10. Fed dovish recession/crisis

Cuts caused by recession/banking stress signal weak earnings/credit. Yields down but equities may fall.

Korea export cycle deteriorates; KRW can weaken due risk-off despite lower US rates initially. Vietnam export demand slows; bank/property credit quality can worsen.

Always ask why policy is easing.

## 11. US long-end term-premium shock

10Y/30Y yields can rise even without Fed repricing because fiscal supply/inflation uncertainty/term premium.

This pressures global duration valuations and mortgages. Korea growth stocks/property-sensitive household conditions can feel tighter. Vietnam foreign valuation and domestic FX room can face indirect pressure through strong USD/global yields.

## 12. USD liquidity squeeze

Global dollar shortage can strengthen USD, widen credit spreads and force deleveraging.

Korea's liquid KRW/equity market may react quickly via foreign flows. Vietnam FX liquidity/foreign access is structurally different, but USD debt/import demand and policy intervention become important.

Track cross-currency funding/credit stress rather than DXY only.

## 13. US recession

US consumption/investment slowdown hurts Asian exports.

Korea has direct tech/auto/export sensitivity. Vietnam electronics/textiles/furniture/FDI manufacturing orders slow.

Domestic policy offset capacity differs; map external vs domestic demand share by company.

## 14. China property stimulus

Property/infrastructure stimulus increases demand for steel, chemicals, machinery and commodities more than consumer brands.

Korea: chemicals/machinery/industrial exporters may benefit. Vietnam: commodity/input prices, tourism/trade and regional sentiment affected; domestic steel/cement may respond depending China supply/export behavior.

## 15. China consumer stimulus

Consumption-focused stimulus favors cosmetics, travel, discretionary/imported products more than heavy industry.

Korean consumer/export brands may respond. Vietnam tourism/agriculture/consumer exporters may benefit.

Do not treat “China stimulus” as one factor.

## 16. China manufacturing subsidy/overcapacity

Subsidies can raise Chinese production and export competition in EVs, batteries, steel, chemicals/solar.

Korean manufacturers may face margin pressure even if input demand rises. Vietnam manufacturers can gain cheaper inputs but face competitive exports.

Demand benefit and supply competition occur simultaneously.

## 17. China slowdown without stimulus

Korean cyclicals/semis/chemicals can face order weakness; KRW sentiment often pressured.

Vietnam trade/tourism/FDI links affected, but China+1 relocation can partially offset over longer horizon.

Separate cyclical slowdown from structural supply-chain shift.

## 18. Oil demand boom

Oil rises because global activity strong. Korea import cost rises but exports may also strong. Net effect sector-dependent.

Vietnam transport/fuel costs rise while industrial/export demand may improve. Upstream oil gains.

Copper/PMI confirmation helps distinguish demand boom.

## 19. Oil supply disruption

Hormuz/war/OPEC surprise may spike oil despite weak demand. Inflation up, growth down.

Korea: trade balance/KRW/airlines/chemicals hit; refiners depend crack spreads. Vietnam: CPI/fuel/logistics pressure; upstream benefits; policy may balance inflation/growth.

This is classic case where “oil up = energy stocks up” is too crude.

## 20. Natural-gas/LNG shock

Korea is energy importer; LNG price can affect utilities/industrial costs and trade balance.

Vietnam power mix/gas-linked projects can face different pass-through structures. Company contracts/tariff mechanisms determine impact.

## 21. Food commodity shock

Food inflation hurts household real income and can influence central-bank policy. Vietnam is exporter of some agricultural products and importer of inputs, creating mixed terms-of-trade effects.

Company margin depends procurement/pass-through, not national CPI alone.

## 22. Copper/metals boom

If driven infrastructure/AI grid investment, Korean industrial/equipment/material exporters may benefit. Vietnam construction/material/resource companies see input/output effects.

If price driven mine disruption instead, downstream manufacturers may suffer.

## 23. AI capex acceleration

Korea is direct listed-market beneficiary through HBM/memory/equipment and power infrastructure. Index impact can be large due weights.

Vietnam benefits more indirectly through electronics FDI, data/power infrastructure and supply chain; listed exposure less pure.

Thus same global AI theme has different beta.

## 24. AI capex slowdown

Semiconductor inventory/order expectations can turn before hyperscaler capex actual declines. Korea may reprice sharply.

Vietnam manufacturing FDI orders may slow with lag; index effect smaller but industrial parks/logistics/electronics names can feel it.

## 25. Memory oversupply cycle

New capacity + demand disappointment → ASP down → utilization/margins fall. Korea earnings/index sensitive.

Vietnam electronics assembly may benefit cheaper components but end-demand weakness can offset. Cross-country supply-chain winners/losers differ.

## 26. Global trade-war escalation

Tariffs reduce trade efficiency, raise some prices and re-route supply chains.

Korea exporters may face direct tariff/localization pressure. Vietnam can gain China+1 FDI but face origin/transshipment scrutiny and US-market concentration risk.

Second-order inflation may keep Fed tighter.

## 27. US-China technology controls

Semiconductor equipment/chips export controls directly affect Korea due supply-chain position and China fabs/customers.

Vietnam can receive relocation in assembly but also face compliance/origin constraints.

Analyze company revenue by China and technology node.

## 28. Geopolitical fragmentation

Friend-shoring/near-shoring moves production from pure cost optimization toward security/resilience.

Korea benefits in strategic semis/batteries/defense but must manage China exposure. Vietnam benefits diversification but needs infrastructure, skilled labor and diplomatic balance.

Capex duplication can raise global cost/inflation.

## 29. Taiwan Strait semiconductor shock

Severe disruption could create global chip shortage but also collapse electronics production. Korean semiconductor producers might appear relative beneficiaries, yet global demand/risk-off/logistics could overwhelm.

Do not trade only “competitor offline = Korean semis up”. Model systemic scenario.

## 30. Shipping chokepoint shock

Suez/Red Sea/Hormuz disruption raises freight, insurance and delivery time.

Korean exporters/importers and shipbuilders/logistics have different exposures. Vietnam exporters with thin margins may suffer freight; ports/shipping can benefit depending rates/routes.

## 31. Global shipping boom

High freight creates shipowner cash but encourages vessel orders. Korean shipbuilders benefit backlog with lag; shipping equities depend charter rates and fleet supply.

Vietnam ports/logistics depend throughput more than global freight rate alone.

## 32. Global banking crisis

Credit spreads widen, USD funding tightens, risk assets de-rate.

Korea: banks/financials and KRW exposed to global risk but domestic asset quality determines depth. Vietnam: domestic banks less directly linked international wholesale funding in same way, yet confidence/foreign flow/FX and domestic credit can tighten.

Policy response architecture differs.

## 33. Domestic Korea housing stress

Household/property stress reduces consumption, raises bank credit concerns and affects BOK reaction.

Vietnam impact may be indirect through regional risk sentiment, not same channel. This illustrates why local shocks shouldn't be copied across countries.

## 34. Domestic Vietnam property stress

Developer bond/liquidity stress hits banks, brokers, construction/materials and domestic confidence.

Korea may see little direct effect except regional EM sentiment. Cross-market correlation can remain low if shock local.

## 35. KRW sharp depreciation

Exporters with net USD revenue can receive translation benefit, while importers/airlines/utilities face cost. Foreign investor KRW return worsens.

For Vietnam, weak KRW can alter Korea-Vietnam corporate investment/trade economics but not mechanically weaken VND.

## 36. VND depreciation pressure

Import-heavy Vietnam firms/foreign-currency borrowers face cost/debt burden. Exporters with USD revenue may benefit.

Korean companies with Vietnam subsidiaries can see translation/change in local costs, but group impact depends natural hedges.

## 37. Foreign-flow shock

Global EM/Asia de-risking can hit Korea fast due liquid market; Vietnam may experience foreign selling but domestic retail/liquidity can dominate index at times.

Same foreign outflow amount relative market turnover/free float matters differently.

## 38. Index-upgrade/technical flow shock in Vietnam

Reclassification can attract passive/active institutional attention but flow occurs by eligibility/weight/timing.

Korea is already developed/emerging classification context depending provider; technical rebalances follow different benchmark structures.

Treat index flow as technical overlay, not economic growth shock.

## 39. Global inflation collapse

Falling commodity/goods inflation plus stable growth can improve Korea import terms and ease VND/KRW policy pressure.

If inflation collapse is due demand crash, benefit from lower rates may be offset earnings weakness.

Again identify cause.

## 40. Global fiscal expansion

US/China infrastructure/industrial spending can lift machinery, semis, metals but raise yields/term premium.

Korea can gain export orders while valuation discount rate rises. Vietnam can gain FDI/trade but strong USD may constrain policy.

Cash-flow and discount-rate channels can oppose.

## 41. Currency translation for investor

Korean investor buying US asset measures return in KRW; Vietnamese investor may think in VND. Cross-border total return combines asset + FX.

Do not compare local-market charts without base currency.

## 42. Cross-asset confirmation

For every shock check:

- 2Y/long yields: policy vs term premium;
- USD/local FX;
- credit spreads;
- oil/copper/gold;
- sector relative performance;
- breadth/flows.

If signals inconsistent, lower confidence.

## 43. Expectations/positioning

Good news can sell off if priced. Korea semis may fall on record earnings if guidance below extreme expectations. Vietnam policy easing may trigger small rally if already expected.

Shock analysis must end with “what was priced before event?”

## 44. Lag structure

FX/yields react seconds; equity sectors minutes/hours; corporate earnings quarters; capex/supply years.

Do not expect economic outcome and asset price to move same date.

## 45. Base/bull/bear shock template

For event create:

Base: most likely magnitude/transmission.

Bull: benign policy response/earnings resilience.

Bear: adverse second-order credit/FX/liquidity feedback.

Then list assets/sectors with direct and indirect exposure.

## 46. Korea vs Vietnam comparison grid

For each shock compare five channels:

1. External trade exposure.
2. FX flexibility/sensitivity.
3. Central-bank policy constraint.
4. Banking/property credit channel.
5. Listed-index sector composition.

This explains why same macro headline produces different index move.

## 47. Avoid deterministic slogans

Never use rules like:

- Fed cut → stocks up.
- War → gold up.
- Oil up → energy stocks up.
- China stimulus → Korea/Vietnam up.

Replace with causal scenario.

## 48. Research note template

`Event → surprise vs pricing → global rates/USD/commodities → Korea KRW/BOK/export/sector → Vietnam VND/SBV/credit/sector → company earnings/valuation → technical flows → thesis update`.

Record forecast AND what actually happened. Over time this builds reaction-function knowledge.

## 49. Mental model cuối cùng

Cross-market analysis is translation problem, not headline reaction:

`One global shock × different economic structures × different policy constraints × different index composition = different asset outcomes`

The edge is understanding transmission and expectations better, not memorizing direction.

## 50. Surprise khác Level

Market thường phản ứng với **difference giữa actual và expectation**, không phải level tuyệt đối. CPI 3% có thể bullish nếu market expected 3,4%, bearish nếu expected 2,6%.

Framework nên là:

`Actual - Consensus - Market Pricing - Positioning = Effective Surprise`

Consensus survey chỉ là một proxy. Futures curves, bond yields, options skew và positioning có thể cho biết market đã price khác survey như thế nào.

## 51. Reaction Function của Market

Cùng một data surprise có thể tạo response khác tùy regime. Strong payrolls trong low-inflation recovery có thể bullish vì earnings; strong payrolls trong inflation fight có thể bearish vì Fed repricing.

Do đó cần học **market reaction function**, không chỉ economic direction. Reaction function thay đổi khi dominant concern chuyển từ growth sang inflation, liquidity hoặc credit.

## 52. Dominant Macro Variable

Tại mỗi thời điểm, market thường tập trung vào một vài variables. Có period CPI dominates, period banking liquidity dominates, period AI capex/earnings dominates.

Một useful question trước event là: “market đang sợ cái gì nhất?” Shock vào non-dominant variable có thể tạo reaction nhỏ dù headline lớn.

## 53. Shock Persistence

Shock có thể transitory, persistent hoặc structural. Temporary shipping disruption khác multi-year trade fragmentation. One-quarter inventory correction khác structural demand decline.

Expected duration quyết định valuation impact. Short shock chủ yếu affects near-term earnings; persistent shock changes terminal margin/ROIC/capex and therefore intrinsic value much more.

## 54. Stock vs Flow Variables

Debt outstanding, installed capacity và inventories là **stocks**; new borrowing, capex, production và inventory change là **flows**.

Markets often turn when flow changes direction even while stock level remains bad. Semiconductor inventories can still be high but price improve if destocking slows. Property debt can remain high but stress ease if refinancing flow reopens.

Distinguishing stock vs flow helps detect turning points.

## 55. Feedback Loop và Reflexivity

Some shocks are self-limiting; others self-reinforcing. Currency weakness can improve exporter earnings but also increase inflation/import cost. Property price decline can tighten collateral, reduce credit, weaken demand and push property lower.

Map feedback:

`Initial Shock → Balance-Sheet Change → Behavior/Policy Response → New Price Move`

If loop is reinforcing, tail risk larger than first-order estimate.

## 56. Balance-Sheet Sensitivity

Same earnings shock hurts leveraged entity more. Country/company with large FX debt, short maturity or thin liquidity buffer can enter nonlinear stress.

Cross-market comparison therefore needs balance-sheet layer:

`Operating Shock × Financial Leverage × Refinancing Need × Liquidity = Equity Tail Risk`

This is why two countries with similar GDP slowdown can have very different asset outcomes.

## 57. Policy Space

Policy response capacity depends inflation, currency, fiscal position, banking health and credibility. Economy with low inflation/stable FX may cut rates/support credit more easily than economy facing currency pressure.

“Growth weak → central bank eases” is incomplete. Ask whether policy has space and whether transmission works.

## 58. Monetary vs Fiscal Offset

A negative external shock can be offset by rate cuts, fiscal spending, credit support or FX adjustment. Each channel creates different winners.

Rate cuts favor duration/borrowers but may weaken currency. Public investment supports construction/materials but can raise debt/term premium. FX depreciation supports exporters but hurts importers/foreign-currency debtors.

Policy mix must be mapped, not summarized as “stimulus”.

## 59. Terms-of-Trade Shock

Terms of trade compare export prices with import prices. Korea as major energy importer can suffer when energy import prices rise faster than export prices. Vietnam has different commodity/export composition, so same oil/metals move can affect national income differently.

Terms-of-trade shock transmits through trade balance, currency, corporate margins and household real income.

## 60. Global Liquidity vs Domestic Liquidity

Global USD liquidity affects cross-border funding, foreign flows and valuation. Domestic liquidity affects local bank funding, margin lending and household asset allocation.

Korea's liquid market often reflects global flow quickly. Vietnam can sometimes diverge if domestic liquidity strongly offsets foreign selling. But prolonged global dollar stress can still constrain FX/policy.

## 61. Cross-Asset Confirmation Scorecard

Instead of binary confirmation, build scorecard:

`Rates: support / conflict`

`FX: support / conflict`

`Credit: support / conflict`

`Commodities: support / conflict`

`Earnings revisions: support / conflict`

`Breadth/flows: support / conflict`

If only equities confirm a narrative while rates/FX/credit reject it, conviction should be lower.

## 62. Relative-Value Signal

Sometimes absolute markets both fall but one country falls less because structure is more resilient. Relative performance can contain information.

Example: Korea vs Vietnam under semiconductor boom, property stress or USD shock. Compare sector-adjusted returns rather than raw indices if composition differs significantly.

Relative-value analysis asks “who is more exposed to this shock?” rather than “does everything go up or down?”.

## 63. Correlation không phải Transmission

Two markets can correlate because common global factor, without direct economic linkage. Correlation can disappear when dominant factor changes.

Causal analysis should identify shared driver: USD, China demand, semiconductors, oil, global risk premium or domestic liquidity. This is more durable than extrapolating historical correlation coefficient.

## 64. Base Currency của Investor

A Korean investor evaluating Vietnam stock has VND asset return plus VND/KRW currency effect. A Vietnam-based investor in Korea has KRW/VND effect.

A market can outperform locally but underperform in investor's base currency. Cross-market allocation should compare total return after FX, tax, cost and liquidity.

## 65. Shock to Earnings vs Shock to Multiple

Some events mainly affect cash flows; others mainly discount rate/risk premium. Fed term-premium shock can compress multiple before earnings change. Semiconductor demand shock changes EPS directly. Governance/access reform may lower risk premium with little immediate earnings change.

Decompose:

`Price Change ≈ Earnings Revision + Multiple Change + Currency + Distribution/Carry`

This makes post-event attribution more precise.

## 66. Earnings Revision Diffusion

Shock starts in exposed sectors then can diffuse. Oil spike first hits airlines/chemicals, later household spending, then banks via credit. AI capex starts semiconductor suppliers, later power/grid/cooling and wages/capex competition.

Track breadth of earnings revisions to see whether theme is broadening or exhausting.

## 67. Flow Feedback

Price rise can attract passive/benchmark/momentum flows, causing further rise even before fundamentals catch up. Decline can trigger margin calls, stop-outs and de-risking.

Flow feedback affects path and volatility, but should not be confused with terminal value. Ask whether flow-induced move creates better/worse expected return from new price.

## 68. Volatility và Optionality

Higher uncertainty raises value of flexibility. Companies with net cash, unused credit and variable capex survive shocks better than highly leveraged fixed-cost firms.

At country level, reserve buffers, policy credibility and fiscal space are forms of optionality. Shock analysis should price both exposure and ability to respond.

## 69. Time-Horizon Matrix

Separate reaction by horizon:

`Minutes/Hours: rates, FX, futures, positioning`

`Days/Weeks: sector flows, valuation, revisions`

`Quarters: revenue/margins/credit quality`

`Years: capex/supply chains/productivity/geopolitics`

A view can be bearish short term but bullish long term if shock triggers capacity exit or structural relocation.

## 70. Pre-Mortem trước Shock

Before important event, write what could invalidate your preferred scenario. Nếu expect dovish outcome, what if inflation composition is sticky? Nếu expect China stimulus benefit, what if stimulus increases competing supply instead of demand?

Pre-mortem reduces confirmation bias after event.

## 71. Post-Event Attribution

After event, record not only whether market rose/fell but why your forecast differed:

`Shock magnitude wrong?`

`Market pricing assumption wrong?`

`Policy response different?`

`Transmission channel weaker/stronger?`

`Positioning dominated?`

This turns mistakes into reusable knowledge.

## 72. Scenario Probability Update

Do not replace thesis completely after one data point. Update probabilities. If base case soft landing 50%, hard landing 25%, reacceleration 25%, a weak payroll may shift weights rather than set hard landing to 100%.

Probability thinking avoids narrative whiplash and forces consistency across new information.

## 73. Cross-Market Hedge Thinking

Hedge should target factor, not country label. Korea semiconductor portfolio may hedge global tech beta/FX differently from Vietnam bank/property portfolio.

Broad index short can leave large basis risk if underlying exposure is sector-specific. Cross-market hedges need sensitivity mapping and stress tests.

## 74. Shock Library phải được Versioned

Historical case is useful only if regime context is stored: starting valuation, inflation, policy rate, leverage, positioning and policy credibility.

Same “oil shock” in different regimes can behave differently. Research note should record context so future comparison is apples-to-apples.

## 75. Unified Shock Template

For every new event, fill:

`1. What happened?`

`2. Surprise vs consensus/pricing?`

`3. Demand, supply, financial or policy shock?`

`4. Expected persistence?`

`5. Global rates/USD/commodities/credit response?`

`6. Korea: trade → KRW/BOK/credit → sectors?`

`7. Vietnam: trade/FDI → VND/SBV/credit → sectors?`

`8. Balance-sheet vulnerabilities?`

`9. Policy offset capacity?`

`10. Earnings vs multiple effect?`

`11. Technical flows/positioning?`

`12. What is already priced?`

`13. Bull/base/bear probabilities?`

`14. What data will confirm/invalidate?`

## 76. Mental model nâng cao

Cross-market analysis is translation plus state dependence:

`Shock × Starting Conditions × Expectations × Balance Sheets × Policy Space × Market Structure × Positioning = Asset Outcome`

The same headline can produce opposite prices because one term in this equation changed. The edge is not memorizing event-response pairs; it is understanding which channel dominates now and updating that model when evidence changes.
