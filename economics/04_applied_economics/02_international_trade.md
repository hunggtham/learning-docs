# International Trade — Comparative advantage, trade costs, firms và distribution

International trade nghiên cứu vì sao countries, regions và firms specialize và exchange; ai được lợi hoặc chịu adjustment cost; và tariffs, trade agreements, exchange rates, logistics hay global value chains thay đổi production như thế nào. Trade theory bắt đầu từ comparative advantage, nhưng applied trade phải đi xa hơn: trade costs, heterogeneous firms, input-output linkages và labor-market adjustment quyết định effect thực tế.

## 1. Comparative advantage

Comparative advantage phụ thuộc opportunity cost, không phải absolute productivity.

Một country có thể productive hơn ở mọi goods nhưng vẫn gain from specialization nếu relative productivity differences khác nhau.

Trong Ricardian benchmark, trade cho phép consumption possibilities vượt domestic production possibility frontier thông qua specialization theo comparative cost.

## 2. Absolute advantage không quyết định trade pattern một mình

Nếu country A cần ít labor hơn country B để sản xuất cả cloth và wine, A có absolute advantage cả hai. Nhưng nếu A đặc biệt productive hơn ở cloth tương đối wine, A vẫn có comparative advantage ở cloth.

Trade pattern dựa relative opportunity cost.

## 3. Terms of trade

Terms of trade là relative export price so với import price.

Trade gain phụ thuộc không chỉ productivity mà còn international relative prices. Large country có thể ảnh hưởng world price; small country thường take world price as given.

## 4. Ricardian assumptions và limits

Simple Ricardian model thường có one factor, perfect mobility domestically, no trade costs và full employment.

Nó giải comparative advantage rõ nhưng không mô tả distribution giữa workers/factors hoặc transition costs tốt.

## 5. Heckscher–Ohlin intuition

Countries export goods intensive in factors they have relatively abundantly, under strong assumptions.

Capital-abundant economy tends toward capital-intensive exports; labor-abundant economy toward labor-intensive goods.

Real data require technology, trade costs, institutions and global production chains beyond simple factor endowment.

## 6. Stolper–Samuelson

Trade-induced goods-price changes can raise real return to factor used intensively in export sector and lower return to scarce factor.

This shows aggregate gains from trade can coexist with domestic losers.

The theorem relies strong assumptions; empirical distribution works through occupations, firms and regions, not only abstract labor/capital.

## 7. Specific-factors model

In short run, some capital/skills are tied to sectors. Owners/workers in import-competing sectors may lose while export sectors gain.

This model captures transition and sector-specific adjustment better than long-run factor mobility assumptions.

## 8. Trade costs

Trade costs include:

```text
transport
insurance
tariffs
customs time
regulatory compliance
information/search
contract enforcement
language
border frictions
```

A small reduction in iceberg trade cost can strongly alter trade when many marginal suppliers are near competitiveness threshold.

## 9. Gravity model

Empirically bilateral trade is strongly related to economic size and inversely related to trade costs/distance.

Stylized gravity:

```text
Trade_ij ∝ (GDP_i × GDP_j) / TradeCost_ij
```

Modern structural gravity includes multilateral resistance: bilateral trade depends on relative ease of trading with all alternatives, not only pairwise distance.

## 10. Border effect

Trade often drops sharply across national borders even after physical distance, reflecting policy, currency, information, networks and institutional frictions.

Border is not one treatment; decomposing mechanism requires detailed data/design.

## 11. Increasing returns and new trade theory

Trade can occur between similar countries because firms have fixed costs and increasing returns plus consumers value variety.

Larger integrated market allows firms scale production and consumers access more varieties.

This explains intra-industry trade beyond comparative advantage alone.

## 12. Heterogeneous firms

Only more productive firms may cover fixed cost of exporting.

Trade liberalization can:

```text
expand productive exporters
shrink/exit low-productivity firms
reallocate market share
raise aggregate productivity
```

This reallocation channel is central in modern trade theory.

## 13. Selection into exporting

Exporters are typically larger/more productive before exporting. Raw exporter productivity premium does not prove exporting causes productivity.

Need designs around trade-cost shocks, market access changes or firm-level entry thresholds to distinguish selection vs learning-by-exporting.

## 14. Extensive vs intensive margin

Trade growth can come from:

- existing exporters selling more existing products;
- firms entering export market;
- new product varieties;
- new destinations.

Policy effects differ by margin.

## 15. Tariff basics

Small-country import tariff raises domestic price roughly by tariff, reducing consumption and imports while expanding domestic production.

Government earns revenue; consumers lose; producers gain; deadweight loss arises from distorted production/consumption.

## 16. Large-country tariff and terms-of-trade effect

A country with market power may lower world export price through tariff, creating terms-of-trade gain that can offset domestic distortion in theory.

This “optimal tariff” result invites retaliation and strategic interaction; it is not a simple policy recommendation.

## 17. Quotas

Import quota restricts quantity. It raises domestic price similarly to tariff under some conditions, but quota rents depend on license allocation.

If foreign exporters capture rents, national welfare differs from equivalent tariff revenue kept domestically.

## 18. Export subsidies

Export subsidy raises domestic producer incentive and can expand exports but costs fiscal resources and may worsen terms of trade.

Support can also provoke countervailing measures.

## 19. Non-tariff barriers

Standards, licensing, local-content rules, procurement and customs procedures can protect legitimate objectives or act as trade barriers.

Identification must distinguish compliance cost from quality/safety benefits.

## 20. Trade agreements

Agreements reduce tariffs and address standards, services, investment, intellectual property, procurement and dispute resolution.

Trade creation replaces high-cost domestic production with lower-cost partner imports. Trade diversion can replace lower-cost nonmember imports with higher-cost partner imports because preferences discriminate.

## 21. Rules of origin

Preferential tariff requires proving product originates sufficiently in member country. Complex rules create compliance costs and can reshape supply chains.

Nominal zero tariff does not imply zero effective trade cost.

## 22. Global value chains

Production is split across countries. Imports can be inputs into exports.

Gross trade statistics double-count value crossing borders multiple times, so value-added trade is important.

Protection on imported inputs can hurt downstream exporters.

## 23. Effective protection

Tariff on final goods and inputs jointly determine protection to domestic value added.

High final tariff with low input tariff can create effective protection far above nominal rate; input tariffs can reduce it.

## 24. Input-output propagation

Trade shock to one industry propagates through suppliers/customers.

Sector exposure should account direct imports plus upstream/downstream linkages rather than only final product competition.

## 25. Trade and labor markets

Import competition can lower employment/wages in exposed industries/regions while export opportunities benefit others.

Adjustment can persist due to geographic mobility, specialized skills and local multipliers.

Aggregate gains do not imply each region recovers quickly.

## 26. China-shock style designs

A common identification strategy uses differential local industry exposure to external supply growth, instrumented by export growth to other high-income countries.

Credibility depends on exclusion and pre-trends; result is local to particular historical shock, not universal effect of all trade.

## 27. Trade adjustment assistance

Policy can insure workers/regions against trade displacement through transfers, retraining or mobility support.

Design should compare trade-specific aid with broader social insurance: displaced workers care about income loss regardless of cause.

## 28. Offshoring

Offshoring relocates tasks rather than entire sectors. It can substitute domestic tasks, lower input costs and expand firms.

Net employment/wage effect depends task complementarity and output scale.

## 29. Services trade

Digital delivery reduces geographic trade cost for software, finance, consulting and remote services.

Regulation, licensing, data rules and language replace shipping cost as key frictions.

## 30. Trade and innovation

Larger market can raise innovation incentives; import competition can induce innovation or reduce rents/resources for R&D.

Effects are heterogeneous by distance from frontier, firm productivity and intellectual-property regime.

## 31. Exchange rates vs trade policy

Currency depreciation changes relative prices broadly; tariff changes specific border wedge.

Pass-through, invoicing currency and global value chains mean exchange-rate changes do not map one-for-one into trade quantities.

## 32. Trade balance misunderstanding

Bilateral trade deficit does not measure bilateral “loss”. Overall current account is tied to national saving-investment balance.

Tariff on one partner can redirect sourcing without changing aggregate deficit if macro saving-investment conditions unchanged.

## 33. Strategic trade and national security

Some sectors have learning spillovers, scale economies or security externalities that can motivate industrial policy.

But identifying genuine spillover and choosing instrument without capture is difficult.

Trade restriction may be second-best if more targeted subsidy/security procurement addresses externality directly.

## 34. Embargoes and sanctions

Trade restrictions can impose economic costs but adjustment through alternative suppliers, evasion and domestic substitution matters.

Causal evaluation needs counterfactual trends and geopolitical co-shocks.

## 35. Carbon border adjustments

A border carbon measure attempts align carbon cost across domestic/import production and reduce leakage.

Design requires measuring embedded emissions and handling trade/legal/administrative complexity.

## 36. Empirical trade toolkit

Common designs include:

- tariff reform differences across industries;
- gravity with policy variation;
- regional exposure shift-share designs;
- firm customs data;
- border discontinuities;
- trade-agreement timing;
- shipping/freight shocks;
- sanctions/embargo event studies.

Trade outcomes are endogenous to economic conditions, so naive trade-GDP correlations are weak evidence.

## 37. Shift-share designs

Regional exposure often combines local industry shares with national/global industry shocks.

Validity requires assumptions about shocks and baseline shares; serial/spatial correlation and common shocks need appropriate inference.

## 38. Welfare decomposition

Applied trade welfare can include:

```text
consumer price effects
producer profits
variety
productivity/reallocation
tariff revenue
terms of trade
adjustment costs
distribution
externalities/security
```

One price index alone is not whole welfare effect.

## 39. Failure modes

Sai lầm thứ nhất là confuse comparative advantage with absolute productivity.

Sai lầm thứ hai là infer country welfare from bilateral deficit.

Sai lầm thứ ba là analyze tariffs without input-output/global-value-chain effects.

Sai lầm thứ tư là use exporter premium as proof exporting raises productivity.

Sai lầm thứ năm là state aggregate gain while ignoring concentrated/local adjustment costs.

## 40. Applied analysis template

Khi phân tích trade question, hãy hỏi:

1. comparative advantage, scale/variety hay firm heterogeneity mechanism nào chính?
2. trade cost thay đổi ở tariff, logistics, regulation hay information?
3. affected margin là price, quantity, entry, product hay destination?
4. imported goods là final goods hay intermediate inputs?
5. incidence theo consumer, worker, firm, region ra sao?
6. policy/shock variation nào identify effect?
7. direct exposure và input-output spillover thế nào?
8. short-run specific factors vs long-run mobility khác nhau không?
9. current account macro identity có bị nhầm với bilateral trade result không?
10. externality/security objective có instrument targeted hơn không?

Trade theory giải lợi ích và specialization; Development Economics hỏi vì sao productivity, institutions, human capital và structural transformation khác nhau dai dẳng giữa economies và households.
