# Development Economics — Poverty, structural transformation, institutions và state capacity

Development economics hỏi vì sao một số societies thoát poverty nhanh, vì sao others stagnate, và policies nào cải thiện productivity, health, education, finance và institutional capacity. Đây không phải “macroeconomics cho nước nghèo”; household constraints, local institutions, market failures và political economy quan trọng không kém aggregate growth.

## 1. Poverty measurement

Income/consumption poverty lines tạo threshold dễ theo dõi nhưng không capture toàn bộ deprivation.

Consumption often smoother and sometimes better measured than income in informal economies.

Multidimensional poverty may include health, schooling, housing and basic services, but weighting dimensions is normative.

## 2. Absolute vs relative poverty

Absolute poverty compares resources to fixed real threshold. Relative poverty depends on distribution, often percentage of median income.

They answer different questions: basic material deprivation vs position within society.

## 3. Poverty gap

Headcount ratio only asks who is below line. Poverty gap measures how far poor households fall below threshold.

Two regions with same headcount can differ substantially in depth of poverty.

## 4. Inequality

Gini, percentile ratios and income shares summarize distribution differently.

High growth can reduce poverty while inequality rises; inequality can fall during stagnation. Distribution should not be inferred from average GDP.

## 5. Poverty traps

A poverty trap exists when low resources themselves reduce future ability to accumulate assets/human capital.

Possible mechanisms:

```text
malnutrition → low productivity
low assets → no profitable investment
credit constraints → underinvestment
risk → low-return safe choices
low state capacity → weak services
```

Evidence must show nonlinearity/persistence, not just correlation between poverty today and tomorrow.

## 6. Credit constraints

Households/firms with high-return projects may lack collateral or formal records.

Credit market suffers adverse selection, moral hazard and enforcement problems.

Observed low borrowing does not prove low demand; it may reflect rationing.

## 7. Microfinance

Microcredit can relax liquidity constraints, but average effects on profits/consumption are often heterogeneous rather than transformative for everyone.

Program evaluation should distinguish borrowing take-up, business investment, consumption smoothing and long-run income.

## 8. Savings constraints

Poor households may face transaction fees, lack secure accounts, temptation/self-control problems or irregular income.

Simple savings products, commitment accounts or payment infrastructure can alter accumulation even without credit.

## 9. Risk and insurance

Farm households face weather, price and health shocks. Without insurance, they may choose lower-return but safer crops/jobs.

Risk can therefore reduce average productivity ex ante, not only consumption ex post.

## 10. Index insurance

Weather-index insurance reduces moral hazard/verification cost but creates basis risk: index may not match individual loss.

Low take-up may reflect basis risk, trust, liquidity and misunderstanding, not absence of insurance value.

## 11. Health and productivity

Health affects welfare directly and can raise labor productivity/learning.

Interventions such as deworming, vaccination, sanitation or malaria prevention may have externalities.

Cost-effectiveness and generalization vary by disease environment.

## 12. Education quantity vs quality

School enrollment can rise without learning gains if teacher absence, weak curriculum or low instructional quality persist.

Development policy should measure learning outcomes, not only years enrolled.

## 13. Returns to education

Private wage returns differ from social returns if education has externalities/signaling.

Access expansion may change marginal student composition and labor-market returns.

## 14. Early childhood

Nutrition, health and stimulation can have dynamic complementarities: early investments may raise returns to later schooling.

Long-run evaluation is important because immediate test effects can understate lifetime outcomes.

## 15. Fertility and demographic transition

As mortality, female education and opportunity cost of childrearing change, fertility often falls.

Lower fertility can create temporary demographic dividend if working-age share rises and labor markets absorb workers.

Demography is opportunity, not automatic growth.

## 16. Structural transformation

Development typically reallocates labor from low-productivity agriculture toward industry/services.

Aggregate productivity growth can come from both within-sector improvement and between-sector reallocation.

## 17. Agricultural productivity

Low productivity may reflect land fragmentation, missing irrigation, credit, risk, input quality, extension information or insecure tenure.

One input subsidy may fail if complementary constraints bind.

## 18. Technology adoption

Farmers/firms may not adopt profitable technology due to learning uncertainty, fixed costs, risk or network effects.

Temporary subsidy can generate experimentation/learning, but permanent subsidy may distort if technology is not socially profitable.

## 19. Land rights

Secure tenure can increase investment by improving expected returns and collateral value.

Formal titling effects depend on enforcement and local customary systems; formalization can also shift bargaining power.

## 20. Urbanization

Cities can raise productivity through agglomeration, matching and knowledge spillovers, but congestion, housing and infrastructure constraints matter.

Urban slums may reflect both poverty and access to productive urban opportunity.

## 21. Agglomeration economies

Firms/workers benefit from thick markets, suppliers and knowledge proximity.

Place-based infrastructure may unlock agglomeration, but subsidies can simply relocate activity from nearby areas.

## 22. Infrastructure

Roads, electricity, water and digital networks lower trade/production costs and expand market access.

Effects depend on complementary institutions and who gains access.

## 23. Market access

Remote regions face high transport cost and thin markets. New roads can raise producer prices, lower consumer prices and shift sector composition.

Empirical designs use road rollout, historical routes or engineering cost variation, but placement endogeneity is a major challenge.

## 24. Institutions

Property rights, courts, taxation capacity, bureaucracy and political accountability shape investment and public-good provision.

Institutions are endogenous to history and distribution of power, so cross-country correlation is insufficient causal evidence.

## 25. State capacity

State capacity includes ability to tax, collect information, enforce rules and deliver services.

Low tax revenue can reflect low income, informality, weak administrative systems or low legitimacy.

Building capacity is dynamic: tax systems may finance public goods that strengthen compliance and growth.

## 26. Corruption

Corruption can distort procurement, licensing and public spending. But broad corruption indices mix perceptions and multiple mechanisms.

Policy should target specific discretion/accountability/information channels rather than treat “corruption” as one variable.

## 27. Political economy

Policies create winners/losers who influence institutions. Inefficient policy can persist because rents are concentrated and reform gains diffuse.

Development economics therefore cannot assume benevolent planner.

## 28. Colonial/historical persistence

Historical institutions can shape land distribution, education, legal systems and state capacity long after original shock.

Historical IV/natural experiments can be informative but exclusion/generalization are often contested.

## 29. Aid

Foreign aid can finance health/infrastructure and provide insurance after disasters. It may also weaken local accountability, create fragmentation or respond endogenously to crises.

Aid effectiveness varies by modality and institution; country-level correlation is not enough.

## 30. Cash transfers

Unconditional/conditional cash transfers can reduce poverty and support investment. Conditionality may raise targeted behavior but imposes compliance/administrative costs.

Cash benchmark is useful when comparing complex in-kind programs.

## 31. Universal basic income

UBI removes eligibility cliffs and administrative targeting but has high fiscal cost if meaningful in size.

Labor-supply, financing and distribution effects depend on what taxes/programs replace it.

## 32. Industrial policy

Government may support sectors with learning-by-doing, coordination failures or strategic spillovers.

Risks include mis-targeting, capture and inability to withdraw support.

A credible industrial policy needs measurable learning/export/productivity targets and exit discipline.

## 33. Export-led development

Exports can expand market size, discipline firms and accelerate technology adoption. But global demand, exchange rates, imported inputs and domestic capabilities matter.

Historical success does not imply identical strategy is available to every country/time.

## 34. Resource curse

Resource wealth can raise income but also increase volatility, rent-seeking, exchange-rate pressure and institutional conflict.

Outcome depends on fiscal rules, ownership and pre-existing institutions.

## 35. Randomized development economics

RCTs identify local interventions well when implementation is controllable.

Limits include external validity, equilibrium effects and focus on interventions that are easy to randomize rather than necessarily largest structural constraints.

## 36. Structural and macro development methods

Some questions require general-equilibrium or dynamic models: migration, trade reform, urbanization, tax capacity or industrial policy.

Reduced-form and structural approaches answer complementary questions.

## 37. Program take-up

Low take-up can reflect price, information, trust, distance, paperwork or social norms.

Diagnosing the friction is often more useful than only estimating average program effect.

## 38. Heterogeneity

Intervention can help constrained households strongly and unconstrained households little.

Average effects can obscure poverty-trap thresholds or differences by gender, geography and baseline assets.

## 39. General equilibrium

Training everyone in one skill can reduce its wage premium. Microcredit at scale can change local competition. Road building can shift firms and land prices.

Scale-up must include market responses.

## 40. Failure modes

Sai lầm thứ nhất là equate GDP growth with broad development.

Sai lầm thứ hai là infer poverty trap from persistence alone.

Sai lầm thứ ba là generalize one RCT across countries/institutions automatically.

Sai lầm thứ tư là use broad institution/corruption index as causal mechanism.

Sai lầm thứ năm là treat structural transformation as painless worker mobility.

## 41. Applied analysis template

Khi phân tích development question, hãy hỏi:

1. constraint chính là credit, risk, health, human capital, market access hay institution?
2. mechanism household/firm/state nào tạo persistence?
3. treatment/policy nhắm đúng binding constraint không?
4. outcome là income, consumption, productivity, learning hay resilience?
5. selection/take-up có làm comparison sai không?
6. design identify local effect nào?
7. complementary constraints có làm program fail không?
8. distribution và gender/geography heterogeneity ra sao?
9. scale-up tạo prices/migration/competition effects gì?
10. state capacity/political economy có khiến implementation khác model không?

Development economics đưa economics về institutional reality: same price signal có thể tạo kết quả rất khác khi credit, state capacity, geography và social constraints khác nhau. Industrial Organization tiếp tục applied logic này ở cấp firms/industries, nơi demand, markups, entry và strategic conduct cần được đo trực tiếp.
