# Public Economics — Taxation, redistribution, social insurance và government provision

Public economics nghiên cứu cách taxes, transfers, public spending và regulation thay đổi behavior, distribution và welfare. Nó nối trực tiếp welfare economics với econometrics: policy không chỉ chuyển tiền giữa groups mà còn thay incentive, prices, labor supply, saving, firm behavior và participation.

Câu hỏi nền tảng không phải “government nhiều hay ít”, mà là: **market failure hoặc distributional objective nào đang được nhắm tới, instrument nào tác động đúng margin, incidence rơi vào ai, và evidence nào identify effect thực tế?**

## 1. Tax wedge và behavioral response

Một tax tạo wedge giữa private return trước và sau thuế.

Ví dụ labor income tax:

```text
Gross Wage − Net Wage = Tax Wedge
```

Behavior có thể phản ứng qua hours, labor-force participation, effort, occupation, compensation form, avoidance hoặc evasion.

Do đó tax base elasticity quan trọng hơn chỉ statutory rate.

## 2. Statutory vs economic incidence

Người legally remit tax không nhất thiết chịu burden cuối cùng.

Payroll tax charged to employer có thể lower wages; corporate tax có thể fall partly on shareholders, workers or consumers depending capital mobility, product competition and horizon.

Incidence là equilibrium question.

## 3. Deadweight loss

Tax distorts choices when marginal private return differs from social opportunity cost.

For small taxes under standard conditions, excess burden often grows approximately with square of tax rate and behavioral elasticity.

But if tax corrects externality, part of “behavior change” is desired rather than distortion.

## 4. Optimal commodity taxation

Ramsey logic suggests raising revenue with lower distortion by taxing less elastic bases more heavily, all else equal.

But distribution and administrative constraints matter. Necessities may be inelastic yet heavily consumed by low-income households.

Efficiency criterion alone is not complete policy rule.

## 5. Income taxation and Mirrlees problem

Government wants redistribution but cannot perfectly observe innate earning ability; it observes income partly determined by effort and skill.

Progressive taxation trades redistribution against behavioral response.

Optimal tax theory depends on:

```text
social welfare weights
income distribution
taxable-income elasticities
extensive-margin participation
administrative/enforcement constraints
```

It does not yield one universal top rate independent of society and data.

## 6. Elasticity of taxable income

ETI measures response of reported taxable income to net-of-tax rate.

It captures real behavior plus avoidance, timing and income shifting.

A high ETI may signal distortion, but policy can lower ETI by broadening base or closing avoidance channels rather than reducing rates only.

## 7. Tax avoidance vs tax evasion

Avoidance uses legal arrangements to reduce tax; evasion violates law/reporting requirements.

Both depend on enforcement, third-party reporting, complexity and opportunities.

Behavioral elasticity is institution-dependent.

## 8. Salience

Consumers/workers may respond differently if tax is visible at decision time.

A tax included in posted price can affect demand differently from one added at checkout even with same economic cost if attention is limited.

This connects public economics with behavioral economics.

## 9. Transfers and phase-out rates

Means-tested benefits often decline as income rises, creating effective marginal tax rates.

If several programs phase out together, combined wedge can be much larger than any statutory tax rate.

Policy analysis should calculate full budget constraint.

## 10. Universal vs targeted transfers

Targeting directs resources to high-need groups but requires information and creates phase-out/administrative costs.

Universal programs reduce exclusion and stigma but spend more on households with low marginal social benefit.

Optimal choice depends on fiscal capacity, measurement quality and distribution goals.

## 11. Social insurance

Unemployment insurance, disability insurance, health insurance and pensions pool risks private markets may handle poorly because of adverse selection, moral hazard or missing markets.

Insurance value must be weighed against incentive effects.

## 12. Unemployment insurance

More generous UI smooths consumption and allows longer search, potentially improving match quality. It can also reduce search intensity and increase unemployment duration.

A complete welfare analysis asks:

```text
consumption-smoothing gain
+ match-quality gain
− fiscal cost
− behavioral moral-hazard cost
```

Effect may vary over business cycle.

## 13. Disability insurance

Disability programs protect workers with limited capacity but eligibility screening is difficult because health/work ability is partly unobservable.

Strict screening reduces false positives but risks excluding genuinely disabled applicants.

## 14. Pensions

Public pensions provide old-age insurance and may redistribute across cohorts/incomes.

Retirement age and benefit formula affect labor supply near retirement, saving and fiscal sustainability.

Pay-as-you-go systems depend on demographic and wage base; funded systems face asset-market risk.

## 15. Health insurance

Insurance protects against catastrophic expenditure but lowers marginal price of care, creating utilization response.

Not all increased utilization is waste: insurance may enable high-value treatment previously unaffordable.

Empirical work must separate price elasticity from health benefit and provider incentives.

## 16. Provider payment

Fee-for-service rewards quantity; capitation creates incentive to economize; bundled payment changes episode-level incentives.

Payment systems should be evaluated for quantity, quality, selection and coding/gaming.

## 17. Public goods

Government provision can address non-excludability/free riding, but financing and production need not both be public.

Applied public economics estimates willingness to pay, spillovers, crowd-out of private provision and distribution.

## 18. Education policy

Education may have human-capital returns, signaling, peer effects and externalities.

Policies include class size, school finance, admissions, vouchers and early-childhood programs.

Identification often uses lotteries, funding formulas, enrollment cutoffs or policy changes.

## 19. Public finance and local governments

Local governments face mobility of households/firms and tax-base competition. Property taxes, school quality and housing prices interact.

Capitalization means public-service changes can show up in land/house values rather than only current residents' flows.

## 20. Fiscal federalism

Which level of government should provide/tax depends on spillovers, mobility, redistribution and information.

Local provision uses local information but can underprovide goods with cross-border spillovers; central provision can equalize but be less tailored.

## 21. Intergovernmental grants

Transfers between governments can be unconditional or matching.

Matching grants lower local price of spending and can induce more provision than equivalent lump-sum transfer.

Flypaper effect describes grant money sticking where it hits more than simple fungibility models predict.

## 22. Environmental taxation

Carbon/energy taxes combine externality correction with revenue raising.

Incidence depends on energy expenditure shares, pass-through and regional production exposure.

Recycling revenue through transfers or tax cuts changes distribution substantially.

## 23. Corporate taxation

Corporate income tax affects investment, location, financing, profit shifting and organizational form.

Statutory rate is not effective marginal tax rate.

International mobility and intangible profits make incidence and base erosion dynamic.

## 24. Consumption taxes

VAT/sales taxes tax consumption rather than income directly. They can be broad and enforceable via invoice chains but may look regressive relative to current income.

Lifetime-income or transfer-adjusted incidence can differ.

## 25. Wealth and property taxes

Property taxes target relatively immobile base but can capitalize into property values.

Net wealth taxes face valuation, liquidity and avoidance issues for private businesses/assets.

Estate/inheritance taxation affects wealth transmission and may influence avoidance/gifting timing.

## 26. Tax expenditure

Deductions, exclusions and credits are economically similar to spending programs delivered through tax code.

Distributional incidence depends on refundability and who has qualifying tax base.

## 27. Behavioral public finance

If people exhibit present bias, limited attention or complexity costs, defaults/simplification may improve take-up or saving.

But behavioral intervention also requires evidence about welfare criterion and heterogeneity; “nudge” is not automatically benign.

## 28. Administrative burden

Application forms, documentation, waiting time and recertification can screen eligibility but also exclude intended beneficiaries.

Take-up is an outcome, not just implementation detail.

## 29. Benefit take-up

Low participation may reflect lack of information, stigma, complexity or transaction costs.

Randomized outreach/simplification can identify barriers, but scale-up may stress administrative capacity.

## 30. Policy evaluation designs

Common applied designs include:

- tax notches/kinks and RDD;
- randomized information/take-up interventions;
- regional or cohort policy DiD;
- eligibility thresholds;
- lottery-based public programs;
- bunching around tax schedules;
- reform timing and event studies.

Each identifies behavior at a particular margin.

## 31. Bunching

If tax schedule has kink/notch, taxpayers may cluster at threshold. Excess mass can reveal behavioral elasticity or optimization frictions.

But measurement heaping, enforcement and knowledge of schedule complicate interpretation.

## 32. Distributional incidence

Average effect can hide differences by income, wealth, family structure, age, region and race/ethnicity where relevant in a study context.

Public economics should report who pays, who receives and who changes behavior.

## 33. Dynamic effects

Tax/transfer policy can change human capital, fertility, migration, firm location and savings over years.

Short-run estimates may miss long-run supply response.

## 34. Fiscal externalities

A program can change government revenue/spending elsewhere: training raises taxable earnings; preventive care changes future health spending.

Cost-benefit analysis should include fiscal spillovers separately from social benefits to avoid double counting.

## 35. Failure modes

Sai lầm thứ nhất là equate legal payer with economic incidence.

Sai lầm thứ hai là use tax rate without effective budget constraint/phase-outs.

Sai lầm thứ ba là treat observed program participation as random.

Sai lầm thứ tư là count every behavioral response as social loss even when correcting externality.

Sai lầm thứ năm là convert one local elasticity directly into universal optimal tax recommendation.

## 36. Applied analysis template

Khi phân tích public policy, hãy hỏi:

1. market failure, insurance problem hay distribution objective là gì?
2. instrument tác động margin nào?
3. statutory và economic incidence khác nhau ra sao?
4. behavioral response là real, avoidance hay reporting?
5. estimand cần ATE, ATT, elasticity hay welfare effect?
6. policy assignment nào cho identification?
7. administrative burden/take-up có matter không?
8. short-run và long-run response khác nhau thế nào?
9. distribution and fiscal externalities ra sao?
10. scale-up/equilibrium effects có làm result đổi không?

Public economics cho thấy policy design luôn là combination của incentives, information, administration và distribution. International Trade mở rộng analysis sang comparative advantage, trade costs, firm heterogeneity và distribution giữa sectors/regions/countries.
