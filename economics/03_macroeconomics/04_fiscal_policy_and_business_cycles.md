# Fiscal Policy & Business Cycles — Demand, multipliers, debt và stabilization

Fiscal policy thay đổi government spending, taxes và transfers. Nó có thể ổn định aggregate demand trong recession, bảo hiểm household income hoặc finance public investment; đồng thời tạo financing needs, distribution effects và long-run incentive trade-offs. Một deficit không tự nói policy tốt hay xấu: cần biết cycle position, interest-growth relation, spending composition và institutional credibility.

## 1. Aggregate demand và short-run output

Trong simple Keynesian framing:

```text
Y = C + I + G + NX
```

Nếu prices/wages sticky và resources idle, fall in private demand can reduce output and employment rather than only prices.

Fiscal expansion can partially fill demand gap. If economy already near capacity, more demand may crowd out private activity or raise inflation.

## 2. Consumption function and MPC

Stylized consumption:

```text
C = C0 + c(Y − T)
```

`c` is marginal propensity to consume (MPC). A higher MPC means transfers/tax cuts translate more strongly into near-term spending.

But households differ. Liquidity-constrained households may have high MPC; wealthy households may save more of temporary transfer.

## 3. Simple spending multiplier

In the simplest closed-economy fixed-price model:

```text
Multiplier = 1 / (1 − c)
```

A government-spending increase raises income, which induces more consumption, creating rounds of demand.

This is a teaching benchmark. Real multipliers are smaller or larger depending on imports, taxes, monetary response, slack, expectations and financing.

## 4. Tax and transfer multipliers

Tax cut raises disposable income, but only the consumed share directly boosts demand. Transfer to high-MPC households may have stronger short-run effect than same transfer to low-MPC households.

Composition matters as much as headline fiscal size.

## 5. Automatic stabilizers

Progressive taxes and unemployment benefits automatically moderate cycles without new legislation.

In downturn, tax revenue falls and transfers rise, supporting disposable income. In boom, reverse happens.

Automatic stabilizers act faster than discretionary packages but may be too weak for exceptional shocks.

## 6. Discretionary fiscal policy and lags

Fiscal policy faces:

```text
recognition lag
legislative lag
implementation lag
impact lag
```

Infrastructure can have high long-run return but slow rollout. Fast transfers may stabilize demand quickly but add less productive capacity.

## 7. Multiplier depends on slack

When unemployment high and firms have unused capacity, demand can raise real output strongly. Near full capacity, same stimulus more likely raises prices and interest rates.

Therefore a multiplier is state-dependent, not one universal constant.

## 8. Monetary-fiscal interaction

If central bank offsets fiscal expansion by raising rates aggressively, multiplier is smaller. If policy rate is constrained at lower bound or central bank accommodates demand recovery, multiplier may be larger.

This is why fiscal effect cannot be analyzed independently of monetary regime.

## 9. Open-economy leakage

Part of fiscal stimulus may increase imports, transferring demand abroad. Flexible exchange rate and capital flows can further alter effect.

Small open economies often have lower domestic multiplier unless policy changes exchange rate or trade response differently.

## 10. Crowding out

Government borrowing can raise interest rates or absorb financing that private investment would otherwise use, especially near capacity or when monetary policy tightens.

But in deep recession with weak private borrowing, crowding out may be small; fiscal expansion can even crowd in investment by raising expected demand.

## 11. Ricardian equivalence as benchmark

Ricardian equivalence says forward-looking households may save a debt-financed tax cut because they anticipate future taxes, leaving demand unchanged.

The result requires strong assumptions: perfect capital markets, rational foresight, lump-sum taxes, long horizons and no borrowing constraints.

Real households violate many assumptions, so theorem is a benchmark about intertemporal budget constraints, not a universal empirical law.

## 12. Government budget constraint

Simplified debt dynamics:

```text
B_t = (1 + r)B_{t−1} + Primary Deficit_t
```

Relative to GDP:

```text
Δ(B/Y) depends on
primary balance + (r − g) × debt ratio
```

If nominal growth `g` exceeds effective interest rate `r`, debt ratio is easier to stabilize for a given primary balance.

## 13. Debt sustainability is path-dependent

High debt can be sustainable with credible taxes, long maturity, domestic currency and strong growth; lower debt can still trigger crisis if foreign-currency exposure, rollover risk or political credibility weak.

Debt ratio alone is not enough.

## 14. Fiscal space

Fiscal space is capacity to use fiscal policy without creating unacceptable financing/inflation risk.

It depends on tax capacity, institutions, monetary regime, currency denomination, maturity, investor base and growth prospects.

## 15. Public investment vs current spending

Government spending that raises future productive capacity can affect both demand now and supply later.

Infrastructure, research or health investment may have long-run returns, but project selection and execution quality matter. Calling every expenditure “investment” does not make it productive.

## 16. Fiscal insurance and distribution

Transfers can stabilize household consumption when private insurance/credit markets fail. Social insurance may also reduce precautionary saving and improve risk sharing.

But program design changes labor-supply and take-up incentives. Distribution and efficiency need joint analysis.

## 17. Supply-side fiscal policy

Taxes affect labor, saving, investment, entrepreneurship and avoidance. Tax base design matters more than marginal rate in isolation.

A reform can broaden base while lowering rate, or shift burden across income, consumption, property and externalities.

## 18. Business cycle as co-movement

Business cycle is not one variable. Output, employment, consumption, investment, credit and profits often co-move with different volatility and timing.

Investment is usually more volatile than consumption; durable goods and inventories amplify cycles.

## 19. Inventory cycle

Unexpected demand weakness creates inventory accumulation; firms cut future production to reduce stock, amplifying downturn. Strong demand can create inventory drawdown and restocking boom.

Inventory is small share of GDP but large contributor to short-run changes.

## 20. Accelerator mechanism

If desired capital depends on expected output, small change in growth expectations can create large swing in investment.

This helps explain why investment is more cyclical than consumption.

## 21. Financial accelerator

Falling asset prices and income weaken borrower net worth, raising external-finance premium and tightening credit. Lower investment then weakens economy further.

Balance-sheet feedback can amplify ordinary demand shock into severe recession.

## 22. Real business cycle perspective

RBC models emphasize real shocks such as productivity and optimize intertemporal responses with flexible prices.

They are useful for disciplined dynamic reasoning but have difficulty explaining some unemployment/monetary phenomena without added frictions.

## 23. New Keynesian perspective

New Keynesian models add nominal rigidities and imperfect competition to intertemporal optimization. This creates short-run role for monetary/fiscal stabilization.

The important lesson is not choosing one “school” by label; it is understanding which friction makes policy non-neutral.

## 24. Demand vs supply shock

Demand shock moves output and inflation often in same direction; adverse supply shock can move output down while inflation up.

Policy response differs because stabilizing inflation after supply shock may worsen output loss.

## 25. Pandemic-style shock

A pandemic can combine supply constraints, sector-specific demand collapse, transfers, reopening demand and logistics bottlenecks. Single demand/supply label may be insufficient.

Macro analysis should decompose phases and mechanisms rather than force whole episode into one curve shift.

## 26. Fiscal dominance and inflation

If persistent deficits cannot be financed through future primary surpluses and monetary authority must accommodate, fiscal conditions can undermine price stability.

But deficit itself does not mechanically cause inflation; spare capacity, financing structure, credibility and monetary response matter.

## 28. Failure modes

Sai lầm thứ nhất là dùng một fiscal multiplier cố định cho mọi trạng thái.

Sai lầm thứ hai là coi deficit và debt là cùng một variable.

Sai lầm thứ ba là nói crowding out luôn xảy ra cùng magnitude.

Sai lầm thứ tư là đánh giá debt sustainability chỉ bằng debt/GDP.

Sai lầm thứ năm là bỏ monetary response khi ước tính fiscal impact.

## 29. Mental model

Khi phân tích fiscal/business cycle, hãy hỏi:

1. Economy đang dưới hay gần potential?
2. Shock demand hay supply, temporary hay persistent?
3. Household recipients có MPC khác nhau không?
4. Monetary policy sẽ accommodate hay offset?
5. Imports/capital flows tạo leakage nào?
6. Spending là transfer, current purchase hay productive investment?
7. Deficit thay đổi debt maturity/currency structure ra sao?
8. `r − g` và primary balance trend thế nào?
9. Balance-sheet/credit feedback có amplify shock không?
10. Short-run stabilization có ảnh hưởng long-run capacity không?

Domestic stabilization vẫn chưa đủ trong open economy. Trade balance, capital flows và exchange rates nối saving/investment decisions giữa các countries và tạo thêm policy constraints.
