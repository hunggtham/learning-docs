# Crises, Regime Change & Path Dependence — Stress tests của institutions và economic systems

Economic crises reveal hidden leverage, weak institutions and political constraints that are difficult to see in normal times. But crisis history becomes misleading when every episode is forced into the same template. Banking crises, sovereign defaults, inflationary breakdowns, currency crises and depressions can interact yet begin through different mechanisms.

This chapter uses crises as comparative stress tests rather than a chronology of famous events.

## 1. Crisis as amplification, not only shock

A large negative shock is not enough to explain crisis severity. What matters is amplification through leverage, maturity mismatch, bank runs, policy constraints, expectations and institutional responses.

```text
Initial shock
→ balance-sheet losses
→ funding/credit contraction
→ forced sales / unemployment
→ weaker tax revenue / confidence
→ further losses
```

Feedback loops turn disturbance into systemic crisis.

## 2. Banking crisis

Banks are fragile when short-term runnable liabilities fund illiquid/risky assets.

Asset losses can become solvency problem; fear can create liquidity run before final losses known.

Deposit insurance and central-bank liquidity reduce runs but require supervision to control moral hazard.

## 3. Credit boom and bust

Boom periods can weaken underwriting because recent defaults are low and collateral prices rise.

Rising collateral supports more borrowing, reinforcing asset prices.

When prices reverse, leverage makes losses nonlinear.

## 4. Fire-sale externality

Forced sellers ignore how their sales lower market prices and damage balance sheets of others holding same assets.

This is a financial externality and a rationale for liquidity regulation or lender-of-last-resort support.

## 5. Debt deflation

Falling prices/incomes raise real burden of nominal debt, causing deleveraging and further spending contraction.

Deflation is especially damaging when leverage high.

## 6. Sovereign debt crisis

Debt sustainability depends primary balance, growth, interest rates, maturity, currency denomination and political capacity.

Default risk can raise yields, worsening sustainability and creating self-reinforcing dynamics.

## 7. Domestic vs foreign-currency debt

Own-currency sovereign has monetary options unavailable to foreign-currency borrower, but inflation and credibility constraints remain.

Foreign-currency debt creates direct balance-sheet risk under depreciation.

## 8. Currency crisis

A fixed/managed exchange rate can become vulnerable when reserves fall, policy inconsistent or defense costs politically unacceptable.

Expectations can accelerate reserve loss.

## 9. Twin crises

Bank and currency crises often interact through FX liabilities and state guarantees.

Depreciation worsens bank/corporate balance sheets; bank rescue worsens fiscal credibility.

## 10. Inflationary fiscal crisis

Persistent fiscal financing needs can pressure monetary system if tax/debt capacity inadequate.

High inflation episodes often reflect interaction of fiscal stress, monetary accommodation, supply disruption and credibility breakdown.

## 11. Hyperinflation

Hyperinflation is not simply “high money growth” as an isolated mechanism. Fiscal collapse, war/reparations, tax failure and currency confidence often interact.

Stabilization generally requires fiscal as well as monetary regime change.

## 12. Depression vs ordinary recession

Deep depressions combine severe demand/output decline with financial distress, debt problems, policy constraints or institutional breakdown.

Using “recession” as one homogeneous treatment hides nonlinear crisis effects.

## 13. Gold-standard constraint

Under gold convertibility, monetary expansion may be limited by reserve/credibility concerns.

Countries leaving restrictive peg earlier can sometimes recover faster, but selection and international conditions must be analyzed historically.

## 14. Bank holiday and guarantee

Emergency closure/guarantee can stop runs but redistributes risk toward state and can freeze payments.

Crisis policy trades immediate stabilization against moral hazard/fiscal exposure.

## 15. Capital controls

Controls can slow capital flight and preserve policy autonomy but distort financing and create evasion.

Effectiveness depends enforcement and whether underlying solvency problem is addressed.

## 16. Debt restructuring

Restructuring reduces debt burden but imposes losses on creditors and can affect future market access.

Delay can preserve hope or worsen debt overhang depending fundamentals.

## 17. Debt overhang

When expected future returns accrue mainly to creditors/taxes, borrowers may underinvest.

Debt relief can improve incentives if debt truly overhangs productive investment, but creates redistribution and precedent effects.

## 18. Austerity and stimulus in crisis

Fiscal consolidation may restore confidence/debt sustainability but weaken demand when multiplier high.

Expansion can stabilize output but be constrained by financing/inflation risk.

Historical effect depends monetary regime, exchange rate, slack and external financing.

## 19. Lender of last resort vs bailout

Liquidity lending against collateral differs conceptually from recapitalizing insolvent institution.

The latter creates fiscal transfer/ownership risk and stronger moral-hazard questions.

## 20. Resolution institutions

Bank resolution aims keep critical functions while allocating losses to equity/creditors rather than uncontrolled collapse.

Credible resolution changes ex ante risk-taking incentives.

## 21. Crisis-induced reform

Crises can create political window for regulation, fiscal reform or institutional redesign.

But reform may reflect panic and overcorrect; stability later encourages deregulation.

Institutional cycles emerge from changing salience and coalitions.

## 22. Political distribution

Crisis losses are allocated among debtors, creditors, taxpayers, workers and asset owners.

Distribution shapes political support for inflation, default, bailout or austerity.

Policy cannot be understood as technocratic optimization alone.

## 23. Unemployment and political effects

Mass unemployment can alter trust, party systems, migration and support for institutional change.

Causal claims from historical crises require careful local variation and pre-trend analysis.

## 24. War and economic institutions

War destroys capital and trade but can accelerate taxation, mass production, technology and state capacity.

Effects are heterogeneous and morally/socially catastrophic; economic institutional consequences should not be confused with welfare gains.

## 25. Reconstruction

Postwar/postcrisis rapid growth may reflect catch-up, unused capacity, institutional reset and capital rebuilding.

High growth during reconstruction is not permanent steady-state growth rate.

## 26. Regime change

Monetary, fiscal, trade or political regime changes alter expectations and behavioral parameters.

Estimating one stable coefficient across regimes can fail Lucas-critique-style because policy rules change behavior.

## 27. Lucas critique intuition

Reduced-form relationships estimated under one policy regime may change when policy rule changes because agents adapt expectations/actions.

Historical policy evaluation therefore needs structural/institutional context.

## 28. Path dependence

Path dependence means early events/institutions alter returns to later choices, making history affect equilibrium selection.

Mechanisms include increasing returns, network effects, sunk investments, organizations and norms.

Persistence alone is not sufficient proof.

## 29. Lock-in

A technology/institution can persist because switching cost and coordination make alternative individually unattractive even if collectively superior.

But apparent lock-in may actually reflect hidden benefits; counterfactual comparison is required.

## 30. Hysteresis

Temporary crisis can leave persistent economic effects via skill loss, firm destruction, lower investment, migration or institutional distrust.

This links macro hysteresis to historical persistence.

## 31. Multiple equilibria

Coordination/complementarity can allow several self-consistent outcomes. Crisis or reform can shift beliefs and move system between equilibria.

Multiple-equilibrium stories are difficult to test and should specify mechanism/predictions.

## 32. Comparative crisis analysis

Compare episodes along dimensions:

```text
leverage
currency denomination
banking structure
exchange-rate regime
fiscal capacity
central-bank tools
external balance
political institutions
international support
```

This is more useful than superficial similarity by decade or asset bubble.

## 33. Historical data limitations

Old bank balance sheets, prices and employment may have inconsistent definitions and survivor bias.

Measurement uncertainty should be explicit.

## 34. Narrative identification

Archival records help classify policy intentions/shocks, but researcher judgment is part of design.

Transparent coding and alternative interpretations matter.

## 35. Difference-in-differences in history

Regional exposure to bank failures, gold-standard exit, war destruction or policy rollout can provide DiD variation.

Parallel trends, migration and spillovers are still required assumptions.

## 36. Instrumental variables in history

Historical instruments often exploit geography, routes or inherited institutions.

Long causal chains make exclusion restrictions especially fragile; mechanism should be traced, not only first-stage strength.

## 37. External validity

A credible estimate from 1930s banking or 19th-century railways may not transport directly to current institutions.

Historical evidence is most valuable for mechanisms and boundary conditions, not mechanical parameter reuse.

## 38. Failure modes

Sai lầm thứ nhất là explain every crisis with “irrational bubble”.

Sai lầm thứ hai là use same policy conclusion across different monetary/fiscal regimes.

Sai lầm thứ ba là call persistence path dependence without increasing-return/lock-in mechanism.

Sai lầm thứ tư là ignore distribution/politics of crisis response.

Sai lầm thứ năm là transport historical causal estimate directly to modern context without institutional comparison.

## 39. Analysis template

Khi phân tích crisis/regime history, hãy hỏi:

1. initial shock là gì?
2. leverage/maturity/currency mismatch nào amplify?
3. banking-sovereign-currency feedback có tồn tại không?
4. policy tools bị regime nào constrain?
5. losses được phân phối cho ai?
6. expectations/credibility thay đổi thế nào?
7. reform thay rule/incentive nào sau crisis?
8. persistence mechanism là capital, skill, organization hay norm?
9. historical design identify effect nào, under assumptions gì?
10. modern external validity cần institution nào tương đồng?
