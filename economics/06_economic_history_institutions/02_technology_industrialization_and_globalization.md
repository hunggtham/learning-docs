# Technology, Industrialization & Globalization — Energy, organization, productivity và structural transformation

Industrialization không phải một invention đơn lẻ. Nó là quá trình trong đó energy, machinery, transport, human capital, finance, organization và institutions kết hợp để làm productivity tăng bền vững và kéo labor khỏi agriculture sang manufacturing/services.

Economic history cần tách ba câu hỏi: technology nào khả thi; firms/states có incentive adopt không; và gains được diffusion/phân phối ra sao.

## 1. Pre-industrial growth constraint

Trong Malthusian-style economy, productivity gain có thể nâng population hơn là sustained income per capita vì fertility/mortality phản ứng living standards.

This is a stylized regime, not a claim every preindustrial society was at fixed subsistence.

## 2. Escape from Malthusian dynamics

Sustained per-capita growth requires productivity/human-capital change outpace demographic dilution and alter fertility incentives.

Industrialization, urbanization and demographic transition interacted rather than occurring independently.

## 3. Energy constraint

Biomass/animal power limit scalable energy density. Coal and later fossil electricity expanded usable energy dramatically.

But resource presence alone does not cause industrialization; transport cost, engineering, capital and institutions determine use.

## 4. General-purpose technologies

Steam, electricity, computing and similar technologies affect many sectors and require complementary investment.

Productivity gain can lag invention because factories, skills, standards and organizational practices must be redesigned.

## 5. Invention vs diffusion

Frontier innovation matters, but diffusion often explains large productivity differences.

A country can access global technology yet fail to adopt because capital, skills, management, market size or institutions constrain returns.

## 6. Learning by doing

Productivity can rise through cumulative production experience.

If learning spills over beyond firm, private investment may be below social optimum, providing one rationale for temporary industrial policy.

## 7. Human capital

Literacy, numeracy, engineering and technical education complement machinery and organizational complexity.

Mass education may be both cause and consequence of industrialization, creating endogeneity in historical correlations.

## 8. Factory system

Factory organization centralizes workers/capital, enabling specialization, monitoring and machinery use.

It also changes labor discipline, urbanization and bargaining relations.

## 9. Division of labor

Specialization raises productivity when market size supports narrower tasks.

Transport and trade enlarge market, enabling deeper division of labor.

## 10. Management technology

Productivity differences can reflect management practices, scheduling, quality control and information systems, not only machines.

Organizational capital is difficult to measure but historically important.

## 11. Standardization

Interchangeable parts, technical standards, accounting units and quality norms lower coordination costs.

Standards can expand markets while potentially locking in incumbent technology.

## 12. Transport revolutions

Canals, railways, steamships, roads, containers and aviation lower effective distance.

Market access changes land values, specialization, migration and firm location.

## 13. Railways and causal identification

Railways were often built toward growing regions, so raw growth near rail lines is endogenous.

Historical studies use planned routes, terrain cost or network access designs, each requiring exclusion assumptions.

## 14. Communication technology

Telegraph, telephone and internet reduce information delay, improve price integration and organizational scale.

Lower communication cost changes both market efficiency and firm boundaries.

## 15. Agricultural productivity

Industrialization requires feeding urban population and releasing labor. Agricultural productivity gains can lower food prices, increase rural income and enable migration.

Agriculture is not merely a sector left behind.

## 16. Structural transformation

Development reallocates labor across agriculture, manufacturing and services.

Aggregate productivity change decomposes into within-sector gains plus reallocation.

Premature deindustrialization may occur when manufacturing peaks at lower income levels due technology/trade patterns.

## 17. Urbanization

Cities create agglomeration economies through matching, sharing suppliers/infrastructure and knowledge spillovers.

They also create congestion, disease and housing costs. Net urban productivity depends on infrastructure and governance.

## 18. Demographic transition

Mortality decline often precedes fertility decline. Education, urbanization and female labor opportunities alter child quantity-quality trade-offs.

Demographic dividend requires jobs/capital/human capital; age structure alone is not growth.

## 19. Capital accumulation

Industrial machinery/infrastructure require finance and retained earnings.

High saving helps transition but permanent growth still needs productivity/innovation.

## 20. Firm size and legal organization

Joint-stock forms, limited liability and managerial hierarchies enable larger firms and long-lived capital projects.

But corporate scale also raises agency and competition concerns.

## 21. Patent institutions

Patent protection can increase invention incentives but restrict diffusion and follow-on innovation.

Historical effects depend technology and alternative appropriation mechanisms.

## 22. Science-industry linkage

Early invention can be craft-based; later sectors increasingly rely formal science, laboratories and universities.

National innovation systems connect basic research, firms, finance and skilled labor.

## 23. Trade and industrialization

Trade gives access to markets, inputs and technology but exposes domestic producers to competition.

Export-led growth, import substitution and industrial policy outcomes depend country size, capabilities and global regime.

## 24. Comparative advantage can evolve

Capabilities are not fixed. Learning, infrastructure, skills and firm entry can change comparative advantage over time.

This creates tension between static gains from specializing today and dynamic capability building.

## 25. Infant-industry argument

Temporary protection/subsidy can be justified if sector has learning spillovers and private firms cannot capture them.

Policy requires identifying real learning externality, not merely young industry.

Failure modes include permanent protection and capture.

## 26. Globalization waves

Global integration varies with transport, technology, capital controls, migration policy and geopolitics.

Trade/GDP alone does not summarize globalization; capital, people, knowledge and value chains matter.

## 27. Migration

Mass migration reallocates labor toward higher-productivity locations and transmits knowledge/remittances.

It also changes wages, housing and politics in origin/destination societies.

## 28. Capital mobility

International finance funds infrastructure and trade but can create sudden-stop vulnerability if short-term/foreign-currency debt dominates.

Historical capital-market integration is regime-dependent.

## 29. Colonial trade and extraction

Colonial systems altered trade routes, land rights, taxation and labor institutions.

Economic legacies differ by colony and institution; analysis should identify mechanisms rather than use “colonialism” as one uniform treatment.

## 30. Forced labor and coercive institutions

Coercive labor can generate output/rents while weakening human capital, mobility and inclusive institutions.

Long-run persistence claims require careful historical identification and alternative-channel analysis.

## 31. Global value chains

Modern production fragments tasks internationally. Technology and logistics allow countries/firms to enter stages rather than whole industries.

Value capture depends capabilities, bargaining and upgrading.

## 32. Deindustrialization

Manufacturing employment share can fall because manufacturing productivity grows faster, consumption shifts to services, or production moves abroad.

Declining employment share does not necessarily mean declining manufacturing output.

## 33. Service productivity

Services range from low-productivity local work to scalable digital/financial/professional services.

Treating “services” as one sector hides major differences.

## 34. Automation and labor displacement

Technology substitutes tasks while creating new demand/tasks. Short-run displacement can be concentrated even if long-run aggregate productivity gains.

Institutions for mobility, training and insurance shape adjustment.

## 35. Productivity slowdown

Measured productivity can slow due weak innovation, diffusion, investment, demographics, mismeasurement or sector composition.

Historical diagnosis should compare competing mechanisms.

## 36. Great divergence questions

Why industrialization began earlier in some regions is contested: wages/energy, institutions, colonies/trade, scientific culture, geography and political competition are among explanations.

A serious account should not reduce a multi-century divergence to one variable.

## 37. Historical natural experiments

Borders, transport routes, war destruction, policy zones or technology rollout can create useful variation.

Historical data quality, migration and spillovers remain major threats.

## 38. Failure modes

Sai lầm thứ nhất là tell industrialization as sequence of inventions only.

Sai lầm thứ hai là infer causal effect from technology adoption where richer regions adopted first.

Sai lầm thứ ba là assume comparative advantage fixed forever.

Sai lầm thứ tư là equate manufacturing employment share with industrial strength.

Sai lầm thứ năm là use one monocausal explanation for divergence.

## 39. Analysis template

Khi phân tích industrialization/globalization, hãy hỏi:

1. technology changes production constraint nào?
2. complementary capital/skill/organization nào required?
3. adoption incentive/barrier là gì?
4. diffusion speed và learning spillovers ra sao?
5. transport/market access thay specialization thế nào?
6. labor reallocation có mobility frictions nào?
7. finance/institutions support long-horizon investment ra sao?
8. trade effect static vs dynamic capability khác nhau thế nào?
9. distribution across regions/classes/sectors ra sao?
10. historical identification phân biệt competing explanations thế nào?

Industrialization tạo sustained growth nhưng không loại bỏ cycles, wars và institutional reversals. Chapter tiếp theo tập trung crises và regime changes như stress tests của economic systems.
