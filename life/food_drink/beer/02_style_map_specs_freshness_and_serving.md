# Beer Style Map, Specs, Freshness & Serving — Đọc một chai bia từ process đến trải nghiệm

Sau [`01_ingredients_mashing_hops_and_fermentation.md`](01_ingredients_mashing_hops_and_fermentation.md), ta đã có chain từ malt đến packaged beer. Bước tiếp theo là biến process đó thành công cụ đọc product thật:

```text
style label
→ process expectations
→ measurable specs
→ freshness / storage state
→ serving condition
→ sensory result
```

Mục tiêu không phải thuộc hàng trăm BJCP-style names. Ta cần một **coordinate system** đủ mạnh để nhìn một beer mới và biết tên style đang nói về fermentation, malt, hops, strength hay tradition nào.

---

## 1. Style là vùng trong process space, không phải recipe duy nhất

Hai beer cùng `IPA` vẫn có thể khác mạnh vì:

- malt/body;
- hop varieties;
- hot-side hopping;
- dry hopping;
- yeast;
- water;
- attenuation;
- ABV;
- packaging/freshness.

Vì vậy:

```text
style name
→ narrows expectation
≠ exact formula
```

Điều này giống classification axis trong Whisky: label hữu ích khi biết nó đang constrain dimension nào.

---

## 2. Sáu axes đủ để định vị phần lớn beer nhập môn

Thay vì một cây taxonomy cứng, dùng các axes:

### Fermentation family

```text
ale
lager
mixed / sour / spontaneous traditions
```

### Malt / roast intensity

```text
pale
amber/caramel
brown/toasty
roasted/dark
```

### Hop expression

```text
low
balanced
bitter-forward
aroma-forward
```

### Strength

```text
session / low
standard
strong
imperial / high gravity
```

### Body / attenuation

```text
dry / crisp
balanced
full / sweet
```

### Special process/material

```text
wheat
smoke
fruit/spice adjunct
barrel aging
sour culture
```

Một style là combination của các coordinates này.

---

## 3. Lager family — fermentation/conditioning axis trước flavour stereotype

`Lager` trước hết liên quan fermentation tradition và conditioning, không đồng nghĩa “pale, light, cheap”.

Lager world có thể gồm:

- pale crisp beer;
- amber/malty lager;
- dark lager;
- strong Bock-family beers.

Điểm chung nằm ở yeast/process family; colour/strength/hop level là axes khác.

Do đó:

```text
lager
≠ pilsner only
```

---

## 4. Pilsner — pale lager nhưng hop/malt balance khác theo tradition

Pilsner family thường có:

- pale malt foundation;
- lager fermentation;
- relatively high attenuation/crisp finish;
- noticeable hop bitterness/aroma compared with many mass pale lagers.

Nhưng Czech-, German- và modern interpretations có water, malt, hopping và bitterness balance khác nhau.

`Pilsner` vì thế là style region, không một sensory point.

---

## 5. Ale family — broad fermentation family, không phải một flavour

Ale includes enormous range:

- pale ale;
- IPA;
- porter/stout;
- Belgian-derived styles;
- wheat ales;
- strong ales.

`Ale` nói rất ít về colour/strength by itself.

Nếu menu chỉ ghi `Ale`, cần thêm style/process information để predict sensory result.

---

## 6. Pale Ale vs IPA — hop emphasis và historical style boundary

Pale Ale là broad pale/amber ale family. IPA historically developed as a more hop-forward branch, nhưng modern market tạo nhiều substyles.

Useful distinction:

```text
Pale Ale
→ usually more balanced / moderate hop intensity

IPA
→ hop expression is central design objective
```

Không dùng IBU alone để define IPA. Modern hazy/NE-style IPA có thể cảm nhận bitterness mềm hơn despite large hop usage.

---

## 7. West Coast vs Hazy/NE-style IPA — process creates different sensory goals

### West Coast tendency

- clearer appearance;
- drier finish;
- assertive bitterness;
- citrus/pine/resin hop expression;
- lower residual sweetness tendency.

### Hazy / New England-style tendency

- high late/dry-hop aroma;
- haze from protein/polyphenol/yeast-process interactions;
- softer bitterness perception;
- fuller mouthfeel;
- tropical/citrus aroma emphasis.

This is not “filtered vs unfiltered” only. Water, grain bill, yeast, hopping and oxygen control all contribute.

---

## 8. Porter và Stout — roast axis, history và overlap

Porter/Stout historically overlap. Modern distinction is not a universal chemistry line.

Common process tendency:

```text
pale/base malt
+
darker roasted grains/malts
→ colour + roast compounds
```

Sensory space can include:

- coffee;
- cocoa;
- toast;
- caramel;
- burnt/char notes.

Stout can range from dry low-strength to sweet/milk stout to imperial high-ABV beer. `Dark` therefore does not imply `strong`.

---

## 9. Wheat beer — grain axis intersects yeast/process axis

Wheat percentage changes:

- protein/haze;
- foam;
- mouthfeel;
- cereal character.

But wheat beer traditions diverge strongly.

### German Weissbier tendency

Specific yeast can produce banana-like esters + clove-like phenols.

### Belgian Witbier tendency

Wheat plus spice/citrus traditions and different yeast/process profile.

### American wheat

Can be cleaner/neutral yeast and hop-oriented.

Thus:

```text
wheat
≠ one flavour
```

Grain axis needs yeast/tradition context.

---

## 10. Sour / mixed fermentation — acidity can come from biological process

Sour beer may involve:

- Lactobacillus/Pediococcus;
- Brettanomyces;
- mixed cultures;
- spontaneous fermentation;
- kettle souring or long mixed fermentation.

Acidity mechanism matters because it affects:

- flavour complexity;
- aging potential;
- microbial stability;
- process time.

Do not treat all `sour` as one style based solely on pH/taste.

---

## 11. Barrel-aged beer — maturation axis added to beer process

Some beers enter spirit/wine/wood barrels after fermentation.

Possible effects:

- wood extraction;
- previous-liquid compounds;
- oxidation;
- microbial contribution depending process;
- alcohol/aroma integration.

This creates a useful cross-link to Whisky cask mechanism, but beer matrix is chemically different and may include active yeast/microbes.

`Bourbon barrel-aged stout` therefore combines:

```text
beer style
+
barrel history
+
maturation process
```

not a new fermentation family.

---

# Part II — Specs

## 12. ABV — alcohol strength, not intensity score

Alcohol by Volume (ABV / 알코올 도수) tells ethanol volume fraction.

Higher ABV can influence:

- warming sensation;
- body;
- flavour extraction/perception;
- shelf stability in some contexts.

But:

```text
higher ABV
≠ more flavour
≠ better quality
```

A 4% pilsner can be technically/sensory complex; 12% beer can be unbalanced.

---

## 13. OG — Original Gravity

OG approximates wort density/extract before fermentation.

It reflects dissolved material derived mainly from malt/cereal sugars and other compounds.

Higher OG gives more fermentation potential, but final result depends attenuation.

Think:

```text
OG = starting resource concentration
```

not “future ABV” exactly.

---

## 14. FG — Final Gravity

FG measures beer density after fermentation.

It reflects remaining extract plus alcohol/water effects.

Used with OG, it helps infer:

- attenuation;
- alcohol production;
- residual body/sweetness tendency.

But sensory sweetness is affected by compound type, carbonation, bitterness, alcohol and acidity.

---

## 15. Apparent attenuation

Simplified apparent attenuation:

```text
(OG - FG) / (OG - 1) × 100%
```

Using gravity notation normalized around water.

Higher attenuation often corresponds to drier/lighter residual extract, but not universally thinner sensory body because proteins/dextrins/carbonation matter.

Use attenuation as process metric, not total mouthfeel score.

---

## 16. IBU — chemical bitterness-related metric, not human bitterness scale

IBU broadly tracks iso-alpha-acid-related bitterness concentration.

Perceived bitterness depends on:

- residual sweetness;
- alcohol;
- sulfate/chloride/water;
- hop polyphenols;
- roast/acidity;
- serving temperature.

Thus 60 IBU stout and 60 IBU pale IPA may not taste equally bitter.

Also extremely high calculated IBU can exceed reliable sensory/measurement usefulness depending method.

---

## 17. SRM/EBC — colour measurement, not roast-flavour score

Beer colour can be reported via SRM or EBC systems.

These measure optical colour under defined methods.

Dark colour correlates with use of darker malt in many recipes but does not directly measure:

- roast intensity;
- sweetness;
- body;
- strength.

A small amount of very dark malt can strongly affect colour without making entire flavour profile “heavy”.

---

## 18. pH — process/sensory/stability dimension

Beer pH affects:

- enzyme/process performance;
- microbial stability;
- flavour perception;
- hop bitterness expression.

Sour beer can have much lower pH than conventional beer, but perceived sourness also depends total acidity/buffering/compounds—not pH alone.

Again, a spec measures one property.

---

## 19. Carbonation — CO₂ level changes aroma and mouthfeel

Carbonation may be expressed in volumes of CO₂ or other units depending industry/context.

Higher carbonation can make beer feel:

- sharper;
- lighter;
- more effervescent;
- more aroma-releasing.

Low carbonation can suit fuller/serving traditions.

Temperature and serving pressure strongly affect dissolved CO₂ behavior.

---

# Part III — Freshness

## 20. Beer starts changing after packaging

Packaging does not freeze beer in time.

Main degradation paths can include:

```text
oxidation
hop-aroma loss
light exposure
microbial change if unstable
staling reactions
CO₂ changes
```

Rate depends beer style, packaging, oxygen pickup, temperature and time.

Therefore “best before” is not a universal cliff where beer suddenly becomes bad.

---

## 21. Hop-forward beer is especially freshness-sensitive

Dry-hop aroma compounds are volatile/reactive.

Over time:

- bright hop aroma fades;
- oxidation can create dull/sweet/cardboard-like notes;
- colour may darken;
- bitterness/aroma balance shifts.

For modern IPA, storage cold and low package oxygen are major quality factors.

A highly rated IPA stored warm for months may show product logistics more than recipe quality.

---

## 22. Dark/high-ABV beer can age differently

Strong stout, barleywine and some barrel-aged beers may develop over months/years.

Changes can include:

- harsh alcohol integration;
- oxidative dried-fruit/nutty notes;
- hop bitterness decline;
- malt/caramel evolution.

But aging is not automatically improvement.

Beer can become:

- oxidized;
- flat;
- overly sweet;
- infected;
- structurally degraded.

`Age-worthy` is product/process dependent.

---

## 23. Temperature accelerates chemical change

Broad chemical principle:

```text
higher storage temperature
→ faster reaction rates
```

Therefore cold storage often preserves fresh beer character better, particularly hop-forward styles.

But freezing can damage packaging/beer and is not normal storage target.

Exact shelf recommendation should follow producer guidance.

---

## 24. Light exposure — bottle colour matters

Hop-derived compounds can react under light and form skunky/light-struck aroma compounds.

Protection broadly:

```text
can / opaque package
→ excellent light protection

brown glass
→ strong protection relative to clear/green

clear/green glass
→ more vulnerable unless hop chemistry/process adjusted
```

This is a packaging mechanism, not a prestige signal.

---

## 25. Oxygen pickup — invisible packaging quality metric

Total packaged oxygen is critical for many beers but rarely visible on consumer label.

Low oxygen helps preserve:

- hop aroma;
- fresh malt profile;
- colour;
- shelf life.

This shows why spec sheet cannot reveal all quality: process control variables may matter more than marketed ingredients.

---

## 26. Can vs bottle vs keg — system trade-offs

### Can

- blocks light;
- light/transport efficient;
- good oxygen barrier when packaged well.

### Bottle

- familiar serving/storage;
- glass inert;
- brown glass offers light protection;
- heavier/breakable.

### Keg

- efficient draft distribution;
- serving quality depends cold chain, pressure, line hygiene and turnover.

Package type alone does not guarantee quality; filling/storage system matters.

---

# Part IV — Serving

## 27. Serving temperature changes perception

Colder temperature generally:

- suppresses aroma volatility;
- reduces sweetness perception in some ways;
- increases crisp/refreshing impression;
- can hide flaws/complexity.

Warmer serving:

- releases more aroma;
- can expose alcohol/sweetness;
- suits some complex strong/dark styles.

There is no universal “beer should be ice cold”. Style and objective matter.

Specific temperature ranges vary; producer/style guidance is more useful than one fixed number.

---

## 28. Glass shape influences aroma, foam and drinking rate

Glass affects:

- head retention;
- aroma concentration;
- visual inspection;
- sip size;
- warming rate.

A tulip can concentrate aroma; narrow tall glass can support carbonation/visual clarity; wide opening changes aroma release.

But glass is second-order compared with beer quality/freshness. Do not turn glassware into ritual absolutism.

---

## 29. Foam is functional, not decoration only

Foam contains proteins, hop-derived materials and CO₂ structures.

It can:

- carry aroma;
- change texture;
- moderate CO₂ release;
- indicate aspects of glass cleanliness/process.

Poor foam retention can come from recipe/process or oils/detergent residue on glass.

Foam height alone is not quality score because styles differ.

---

## 30. Draft system quality can dominate the last meter

A good keg through dirty/poorly balanced draft lines can taste bad.

Draft system variables:

- line cleanliness;
- temperature;
- CO₂ pressure/gas blend;
- line length/resistance;
- keg turnover;
- faucet hygiene.

Thus consumer experience includes distribution/serving infrastructure after brewery packaging.

This is a boundary where How Things Work could own a full draft-system chain if expanded; Life only keeps user-facing mechanism.

---

## 31. Pouring changes CO₂ release and foam

A controlled pour allows CO₂ to leave solution and form appropriate head.

Too gentle can leave excessive dissolved CO₂ and bloated mouthfeel; too aggressive may foam excessively.

Exact technique depends package/style/carbonation.

The important principle:

```text
pouring
→ changes gas-liquid equilibrium and foam
```

not merely presentation aesthetics.

---

## 32. Sediment can be intentional

Bottle-conditioned or unfiltered beer may contain yeast/protein sediment.

Choices:

- pour clear beer and leave sediment;
- swirl/include sediment where style/producer suggests.

Sediment presence is not automatically spoilage.

Need distinguish expected process residue from signs of contamination/package failure.

---

## 33. Sensory comparison needs controlled conditions

If comparing two beers, control:

- serving temperature;
- glass cleanliness/type;
- freshness/package date;
- pour/carbonation;
- order of tasting;
- food/context.

Otherwise observed difference may come from serving state rather than product formulation.

This is basic experimental-control reasoning applied to daily life.

---

## 34. A practical style/spec matrix

| Question | Example signal | What it does NOT prove |
|---|---|---|
| fermentation family? | Ale/Lager/Mixed | exact flavour |
| strength? | ABV | quality/intensity |
| starting extract? | OG | final sweetness |
| final attenuation? | FG/attenuation | total body |
| bitterness compounds? | IBU | perceived bitterness linearly |
| colour? | SRM/EBC | roast strength/ABV |
| freshness? | package date/storage | recipe quality alone |
| serving state? | temp/carbonation | brewery process alone |

Bảng này nên dùng như routing map: mỗi metric mở một question khác, không cộng lại thành quality score.

---

## 36. Drill — same style, different state

Compare the same beer or very similar beer under two conditions:

```text
fresh/cold-stored
vs
older/warm-stored
```

Observe:

- aroma intensity;
- colour;
- bitterness balance;
- malt sweetness;
- carbonation;
- stale/oxidative notes.

Do not overgeneralize from one bottle; use exercise to learn degradation signatures.

---

## 37. Common misconceptions

### “IPA = bitter beer”

Hop aroma/process are central; modern IPA spans bitterness levels.

### “Dark beer = strong/heavy”

Colour, ABV and body are separate axes.

### “IBU tells exactly how bitter it tastes”

IBU is a chemical metric, perception is contextual.

### “Can beer is lower quality than bottle”

Package material alone does not determine product quality; cans solve light exposure well.

### “Beer should always be as cold as possible”

Serving temperature trades refreshment for aroma/expression.

### “Old strong beer always improves”

Aging can develop or degrade depending product/process/storage.

---

## 38. Boundary

This chapter is consumer/process literacy, not brewing production specification. Exact style definitions, competition guidelines, microbiological QC thresholds, draft installation standards and alcohol-service law belong to their specialist/official sources.

For alcohol use, serving size/health risk belong to appropriate health guidance rather than sensory/style chapters.
