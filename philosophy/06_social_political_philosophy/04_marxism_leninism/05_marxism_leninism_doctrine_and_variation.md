# Marxism–Leninism — doctrine, systematization, institutions và variation

Tới đây ta đã tách Marx và Lenin thành hai historical layers. Bước tiếp theo là hiểu điều gì xảy ra khi một contested intellectual tradition được **codify thành official doctrine**, dùng trong party education, state legitimacy, political language và interpretation of policy.

**Marxism–Leninism** vì vậy không nên được dùng như tên gọi ngược cho toàn bộ những gì Marx và Lenin từng viết. It is better understood as a historically systematized framework built from authoritative interpretations of Marx, Engels and Lenin, later institutionalized in Soviet political life and adapted elsewhere.

## From intellectual tradition to doctrine

An intellectual tradition normally contains disagreement. Scholars and activists argue about interpretation, evidence, strategy and institutional design. Once a ruling organization needs an official doctrine, the function changes.

Doctrine must do more than generate inquiry. It may need to:

```text
identify canonical authors
→ define authorized vocabulary
→ organize curriculum
→ explain history
→ justify institutions
→ guide cadres and policy language
→ distinguish orthodox from heterodox interpretation
```

This transformation matters epistemically. A concept can function at once as analytical claim, educational category and legitimacy language. Those functions must be separated when evaluating truth or explanatory value.

## Canon formation: why “Marx–Engels–Lenin” becomes a lineage

Marx and Engels wrote across decades and changed emphasis. Lenin wrote in different strategic contexts. Later systematization tends to reduce this plurality into a more coherent lineage.

This does not mean the lineage is fabricated; there are real continuities. But codification performs **selection and compression**:

```text
large heterogeneous corpus
→ selected canonical texts
→ standardized concepts
→ teachable doctrine
```

Every compression loses some ambiguity and debate. Therefore when a textbook presents a clean proposition, a source-critical reader should ask whether it is direct primary-text claim, later synthesis, or institutional formulation.

## Dialectical materialism: worldview, method, or doctrine?

In later Marxist-Leninist doctrine, **dialectical materialism** often functions as a general worldview and methodology: reality is material, changing, relational and characterized by tensions or contradictions.

The term can be used at several levels:

1. **ontological** — reality is not fundamentally dependent on mind/spirit;
2. **methodological** — objects should be studied dynamically, relationally and historically;
3. **doctrinal** — a set of named laws/categories taught as part of official philosophy.

These levels should not be collapsed. The methodological advice “study systems historically and relationally” is weaker than a universal ontological claim, and both are different from a fixed classroom schema.

A useful discipline is to translate dialectical language into a testable mechanism whenever possible:

```text
contradiction → which variables or relations are in tension?
quantity → quality → what threshold mechanism?
negation → what concrete transformation?
interconnection → which causal pathways?
```

If no mechanism or evidence can be specified, dialectical vocabulary risks becoming post-hoc description.

## Historical materialism inside doctrine

Later Marxism–Leninism commonly presents **historical materialism** as application of materialist-dialectical analysis to society. Productive forces, relations of production, class structure and political superstructure are organized into a more systematic explanatory architecture than one finds in any single Marx text.

The strength of this codification is pedagogical clarity. The risk is **mechanical determinism**: treating social change as a fixed sequence where economy mechanically produces politics and ideology.

A deeper reading should preserve interaction:

```text
productive forces ↔ production relations
production relations ↔ class structure
class structure ↔ political institutions
political institutions ↔ property and production
ideas / ideology ↔ organization and legitimacy
```

This retains historical-materialist emphasis without turning it into one-way reductionism.

## Scientific socialism: historical label versus contemporary epistemic standard

**Scientific socialism (chủ nghĩa xã hội khoa học, 과학적 사회주의)** historically contrasts socialism grounded in an account of social development with utopian blueprinting.

But “scientific” here is not self-certifying. Under contemporary philosophy of science, a social theory's claims still require:

```text
clear concepts
→ operationalization where possible
→ evidence
→ causal or comparative reasoning
→ exposure to counterevidence
→ revision when claims fail
```

This distinction protects two things at once: historical accuracy about how the tradition describes itself, and modern epistemic discipline about what counts as evidence.

## Party leadership as doctrinal principle

Lenin's organizational theory enters Marxism–Leninism as a core principle of **communist party leadership**. The party is commonly described as organized leadership of the working class and political center of socialist construction.

To understand this institutionally, decompose the claim into functions:

```text
representation
coordination
cadre selection
policy formulation
ideological education
state leadership
social mobilization
```

Each function creates a corresponding accountability question. For example, if party claims representation, what mechanism links party preferences to those represented? If party selects cadres, what constrains patronage or information filtering? If party leads the state, where does administrative expertise end and political direction begin?

## Party–state relation: overlap is not identity

A Marxist-Leninist political system may contain both **party institutions** and **state institutions**. They can overlap in personnel, policy direction and hierarchy but remain analytically distinct.

This matters because different channels perform different roles:

```text
party → political line, cadre system, organizational leadership
state → law, administration, budgeting, coercive/legal apparatus, service delivery
mass organizations / social bodies → participation, mobilization, representation channels
```

Actual arrangements vary by country and period. A description that says only “the party controls everything” may be too coarse to explain how decisions are made, while a purely formal constitutional description may understate party influence. Institutional analysis needs both formal and informal authority.

## Democratic centralism inside doctrine

**Democratic centralism** becomes a core organizational vocabulary in Marxist-Leninist systems. The broad principle combines discussion/participation with unified implementation after decisions.

Its real meaning depends on rules, not name. Depth analysis asks:

```text
who participates in discussion?
who controls agenda?
what information is available?
how are leaders appointed/elected?
can minorities preserve positions?
what sanctions follow dissent?
what feedback exists after implementation?
```

Different answers produce very different institutional systems under the same label.

## Transition: socialism and communism as different analytical levels

In Marxist-Leninist vocabulary, socialism is often treated as a transitional social formation, while communism represents a more developed classless horizon.

This creates at least four separate transition tasks:

1. **property transformation** — who owns or controls major productive assets;
2. **coordination transformation** — how production and allocation decisions are made;
3. **class transformation** — how old/new occupational and ownership groups change;
4. **state transformation** — why coercive class institutions should eventually diminish rather than become permanent.

If these are collapsed into one word “transition,” mechanism disappears.

## Planning is not equivalent to public ownership

A doctrinal emphasis on public/social ownership does not logically fix one single coordination mechanism. State-owned assets can be coordinated by detailed command planning, negotiated targets, enterprise autonomy, market prices, competitive procurement or hybrids.

Therefore three variables must be kept separate:

```text
ownership
coordination
political control
```

A system can change one without fully changing the others. China and Vietnam later provide especially important examples of continued party rule and significant state ownership alongside extensive market coordination.

This is why [Historical implementation](06_historical_implementation_and_evidence.md) codes actual institutions rather than inferring them directly from ideological labels.

## Doctrine and policy: do not assume one-to-one mapping

Official doctrine can constrain policy language and legitimate broad goals, but actual policy also responds to:

```text
resource constraints
administrative capacity
war/security
technology
fiscal conditions
international trade
leadership conflict
social resistance
policy failure and learning
```

Thus ideological continuity can coexist with large policy change. Conversely, a policy may be justified using doctrinal language even when its mechanism resembles institutions found in very different ideological systems.

A useful research rule is:

```text
doctrine ≠ policy
policy ≠ institution
institution ≠ outcome
```

Trace each arrow instead of assuming identity.

## National adaptation: transmission is never pure copying

After Soviet codification, communist movements and states adapted doctrine to different historical contexts. Agrarian structure, colonial history, nationalism, war, development level and geopolitical position changed emphasis.

A conceptual genealogy might look like:

```text
Marx / Engels
→ Lenin
→ Soviet codification
→ national adaptation
→ reform / reinterpretation over time
```

This is why the same broad label can coexist with substantially different economic institutions and political practices across Soviet Union, China, Vietnam or Cuba.

## Marxism–Leninism, Stalinism and continuity debate

**Marxism–Leninism** as official doctrine and **Stalinism** as ideas, institutions and practices associated especially with Stalin's period overlap, but they are not perfect synonyms.

The serious historical issue is continuity and discontinuity. Some institutions developed from earlier Bolshevik practices; others intensified or changed qualitatively under Stalin. A responsible analysis therefore avoids two shortcuts:

```text
"Stalin = direct execution of every Marx/Lenin idea"
"Stalin = completely unrelated to Leninist tradition"
```

Both remove the historical work needed to trace institutional development.

## Doctrine as legitimacy language

Once institutionalized, ideology can perform a legitimacy function: explaining why ruling institutions are justified, what historical direction society is pursuing, and how policy sacrifices are interpreted.

This does not mean every doctrinal statement is false because it serves legitimacy. **Function and truth are different dimensions.** A claim can be true and politically useful; false and useful; or contested and useful.

For each doctrinal proposition, separate:

```text
epistemic question: is the claim supported?
functional question: what political/institutional role does the claim play?
```

This distinction prevents ideology critique from becoming a genetic fallacy.

## Institutionalization creates an error-correction problem

When doctrine becomes tied to authority, criticism of theory may be interpreted as criticism of institution. That can create **epistemic lock-in**: actors have incentive to report evidence compatible with authorized categories.

The general mechanism is not unique to Marxist-Leninist states; corporations, bureaucracies and scientific paradigms can also filter unwelcome information. But where political and doctrinal authority are strongly linked, the stakes of dissent may be higher.

An institutional analysis therefore asks whether there are channels for:

```text
negative information
policy experimentation
revision of theory
local feedback
independent verification
leadership correction
```

These variables become central in the Soviet/China/Vietnam case studies later in the module.

## Mental model bàn giao

Marxism–Leninism should now be understood as **a historically codified and institutionalized ideological system**, not a timeless synonym for Marx's complete thought.

Its architecture can be decomposed as:

```text
materialist worldview / dialectical method
→ historical-materialist social analysis
→ theory of capitalism and class
→ party leadership and revolutionary transition
→ socialist construction
→ long-run communist horizon
```

But architecture alone does not tell us what actual institutions did. The next chapter therefore changes evidence type: from textual/doctrinal reconstruction to historical institutional analysis.
