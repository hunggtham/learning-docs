# Tiến hóa, di truyền và hành vi — Evolution, Genetics & Behavior

Giải thích tiến hóa hỏi **vì sao một mechanism/trait có thể xuất hiện và được duy trì qua lịch sử chọn lọc**, còn behavioral genetics hỏi **variation giữa individuals liên quan genetic và environmental variation thế nào**. Hai câu hỏi thường bị trộn thành câu “hành vi X là do gene”, một formulation quá mơ hồ và dễ dẫn tới genetic determinism.

> **Trạng thái bằng chứng tổng quát:** complex psychological traits are polygenic and shaped by gene–environment interplay là nền tương đối vững. Heritability is population/context statistic, not individual causal percentage. Specific evolutionary adaptation accounts are usually hypotheses/current theories that require independent evidence; plausible ancestral story alone is not validation.

## 1. Tinbergen: bốn loại câu hỏi

Một behavior có thể được hỏi ở nhiều level:

- **mechanism**: neural, hormonal, cognitive process hiện tại;
- **development (ontogeny)**: trait phát triển qua life history thế nào;
- **function**: trait có thể ảnh hưởng fitness ra sao;
- **phylogeny**: trait evolved across species lineage thế nào.

Mechanism và function complement, not replace each other.

Ví dụ jealousy có proximate triggers, appraisal và learning history; evolutionary hypothesis about mate retention is separate claim requiring separate evidence.

## 2. Natural selection không có mục tiêu trung tâm

**Chọn lọc tự nhiên (natural selection)** changes frequency of heritable variants when they affect reproductive success under particular environments.

Evolution does not “want” happiness, morality or perfect health.

Anthropomorphic phrase “evolution designed X for Y” can be shorthand, but should not imply conscious planner.

## 3. Adaptation, by-product và drift

A trait may be:

- adaptation shaped by selection;
- by-product of another adaptation;
- consequence of developmental constraint;
- neutral or near-neutral variation;
- culturally acquired pattern.

Not every behavior with plausible function is adaptation.

## 4. Adaptationist storytelling

Weak evolutionary explanation often starts after observation:

```text
Behavior X exists
→ invent ancestral benefit Y
→ conclude X evolved for Y
```

This is post-hoc story unless Y predicts new patterns that distinguish competing accounts.

Good evolutionary hypothesis asks for evidence from:

- comparative species data;
- cross-cultural pattern;
- developmental timing;
- sex/age/ecology differences predicted in advance;
- genetic/physiological mechanism;
- cost–benefit trade-offs.

> **Hypothesis rule:** adaptation claim should create risky prediction, not just plausible narrative.

## 5. Mismatch

An evolved mechanism can function differently in modern environment from ancestral conditions.

Preference for energy-dense food is common mismatch example, but “ancestral environment” was not one static cave. Human ecologies varied widely.

Mismatch is useful framework only when historical ecology and mechanism are specified.

## 6. Behavioral genetics

Behavioral genetics estimates how genetic and environmental differences contribute to variation in traits.

Classical methods include twin/adoption designs; modern methods use measured DNA, genome-wide association studies (GWAS), polygenic scores and family-based genomic designs.

No method is assumption-free.

## 7. Heritability

**Hệ số di truyền (heritability)** estimates proportion of phenotypic variance associated with genetic variance in a specific population/environment/model.

It does **not** mean:

```text
“60% intelligence of this person is genetic”
```

Heritability is not an individual pie chart.

## 8. High heritability does not mean immutable

A trait can be highly heritable and still modifiable by environment/intervention.

Phenylketonuria illustrates this clearly: genetic cause can coexist with strong environmental treatment effect.

Likewise, vision traits can be heritable while glasses change functional outcome.

## 9. Heritability does not explain group differences

This is especially important.

If a trait is heritable **within** group A and group B, this does not tell whether mean difference **between** groups is genetic, environmental or both.

Recent methodological work reiterates that within-group heritability is uninformative by itself about causes of between-group differences.

Therefore using heritability estimate to explain ethnic, national, class or gender mean difference is invalid without much stronger causal evidence.

## 10. Shared and nonshared environment

Twin models often partition variance into additive genetic, shared environmental and nonshared environmental components.

“Nonshared environment” includes measurement error and experiences that differ between siblings. It should not be romanticized as “unique life events” only.

Model assumptions affect estimates.

## 11. Equal-environments and other assumptions

Twin inference depends on assumptions about environmental similarity, assortative mating and gene–environment correlation.

Modern genomic designs provide complementary evidence but bring other issues such as population stratification and ancestry portability.

Confidence should come from converging methods.

## 12. Polygenic architecture

Complex psychological traits are usually **polygenic**: thousands of variants can each contribute tiny effects.

There is no single “gene for intelligence”, “gene for depression” or “gene for aggression” in the simplistic sense.

Genes influence molecular/developmental pathways whose effects depend context.

## 13. GWAS

**Genome-Wide Association Study (GWAS)** tests associations between genetic variants and phenotype across large samples.

GWAS signal is association, not automatically causal variant or mechanism. Linkage disequilibrium means associated marker may tag nearby causal biology.

Large N is necessary because individual effects are tiny.

## 14. Polygenic scores

**Điểm đa gene (polygenic score)** aggregates many variant weights to predict statistical liability/trait variation.

Polygenic score:

- is probabilistic;
- depends discovery sample;
- often loses predictive accuracy across ancestry/context;
- can capture indirect family/social pathways;
- is not destiny.

Use for individual decision requires strong validation and ethical caution.

## 15. Genetic nurture and indirect effects

Parents' genotypes can affect child environment through parental education, behavior, income or home resources even when variant not inherited.

This **genetic nurture/indirect genetic effect** complicates interpretation of genotype–phenotype association.

Gene effect may partly operate through social environment.

## 16. Gene–environment correlation

Three classic forms:

- **passive rGE**: parents transmit genes and correlated environments;
- **evocative rGE**: person's traits evoke responses;
- **active rGE**: person selects environments matching dispositions.

Thus environment exposure is often not random.

## 17. Gene–environment interaction

**Tương tác gene–environment (G×E)** occurs when effect of genotype differs across environments or vice versa.

Modern genomic research confirms G×E is conceptually important, but detection is statistically difficult because exposure measurement, sample size, model scale and replication matter.

> **Current evidence:** G×E exists for complex traits; specific interaction claims require large, well-measured and replicated samples.

## 18. Reaction range is too simple

Old “genes set range, environment chooses point” metaphor is limited because gene expression, developmental feedback and environment selection are dynamic.

Better model:

```text
Genes ↔ development ↔ environment ↔ behavior
```

not fixed range.

## 19. Epigenetics

**Biểu sinh (epigenetics)** refers to molecular regulation affecting gene expression without changing DNA sequence, such as DNA methylation and chromatin processes.

Environment can influence epigenetic states.

But popular claim “trauma rewrites genes and passes exactly to grandchildren” exceeds human evidence. Intergenerational social transmission and direct exposure are major confounders.

> **Established:** epigenetic regulation matters biologically.
>
> **Debated/hypothesis:** strong transgenerational inheritance of specific psychological trauma signatures in humans.

## 20. Gene expression is context-sensitive

A DNA sequence does not prescribe fixed behavior directly. Genes participate in cellular processes whose expression changes by tissue, developmental stage and environment.

This is why “gene for behavior X” is usually misleading shorthand.

## 21. Cultural evolution

Human behavior also changes through social transmission. Norms, language, institutions and technology can spread much faster than genetic evolution.

**Đồng tiến hóa gene–culture (gene–culture coevolution)** studies feedback between biological and cultural inheritance.

Lactase persistence is canonical biological example; psychological domains may also show feedback between social practice and selection pressure.

## 22. Social learning

Culture accumulates because humans imitate, teach, conform, innovate and select models.

Some cross-cultural regularities may reflect shared biology; others shared ecological problem; others cultural diffusion.

Recurring motif alone cannot identify mechanism.

## 23. Evolutionary psychology

Evolutionary psychology studies how selection history may shape psychological mechanisms.

Strong work generates testable hypotheses about information processing, trade-offs and ecological conditions.

Weak popular use simply labels behavior “evolutionary” after observing it.

## 24. Sexual selection

Mate choice and competition can create selection beyond survival.

Evolutionary hypotheses about mating behavior can be useful, but modern gender roles, contraception, economic independence and culture change incentives profoundly.

Cross-cultural variation is evidence to explain, not noise to ignore.

## 25. Kin selection and cooperation

Inclusive-fitness models explain why helping relatives can evolve. Reciprocal cooperation, reputation and norm enforcement can support cooperation beyond kin.

Human prosociality cannot be reduced to one mechanism.

## 26. Prepared learning

Some threat associations may be acquired more easily than others, suggesting evolutionary constraints on learning.

But fear still depends experience and context. “Evolved preparedness” does not mean innate fully formed fear.

## 27. Personality and genetics

Personality traits show heritable variation at population level, but development, environment and measurement matter.

Genetic association does not mean personality cannot change.

Xem [[../03_human_development_and_person/03_personality]].

## 28. Intelligence and genetics

Cognitive-test performance has substantial heritability in many populations, and modern molecular genetics identifies highly polygenic associations.

But:

- heritability changes across age/context;
- education and health matter;
- score is measurement construct;
- group differences cannot be inferred from within-group heritability;
- polygenic prediction is ancestry/context dependent.

Xem [[../02_learning_and_cognition/03_intelligence_and_cognitive_differences]].

## 29. Mental health genetics

Psychiatric disorders are generally polygenic and heterogeneous. Genetic liability overlaps across diagnoses.

Polygenic risk is one factor among development, environment and stochastic processes, not diagnosis in itself.

## 30. Heritability and intervention

A common error:

```text
Trait highly heritable
→ environment intervention useless
```

This conclusion does not follow.

Intervention acts on causal pathway/environment even when variation in current population is partly genetic.

## 31. Moral naturalistic fallacy

“Evolved” does not mean:

- good;
- desirable;
- unavoidable;
- morally justified.

Evolutionary explanation is descriptive, not ethical prescription.

## 32. Race/ancestry caution

Genetic ancestry, socially defined race and ethnicity are related but not equivalent constructs.

Group labels often bundle environment, discrimination, geography, migration and socioeconomic history.

Psychological difference claims require exceptional measurement and causal rigor.

## 33. Common misconceptions

### “Heritability 70% means 70% of me is genetic”

Sai.

### “High heritability means cannot change”

Sai.

### “If two groups differ and trait is heritable, difference is genetic”

Sai inference.

### “Evolutionary explanation means behavior is inevitable”

Sai.

### “Epigenetics proves trauma is biologically inherited for many generations”

Too strong for human psychological claims.

### “A polygenic score predicts destiny”

Sai.

## 34. Evidence-status checklist for evolutionary claims

Before accepting adaptation claim, ask:

1. Is phenomenon reliable?
2. What alternative learning/cultural account exists?
3. Does hypothesis make novel prediction?
4. Cross-cultural/comparative evidence?
5. Developmental mechanism?
6. Cost/trade-off predicted?
7. Could trait be by-product?
8. Is data independent of story used to generate hypothesis?

## 35. Mental model

```text
Genetic variation
      ↕
Development ↔ Environment
      ↕          ↕
 Learning ↔ Culture
      ↓
Behavior / trait distribution
```

For evolutionary history:

```text
Mechanism now
≠ Developmental origin
≠ Evolutionary function
≠ Phylogenetic history
```

These questions connect but need separate evidence.
